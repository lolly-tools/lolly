# Generated music

The Neurospicy player makes its own music. Its generated stations compose every tune as they play. Nothing is recorded or sampled: each tune is written by Lolly's composer and played by instruments synthesised in your browser.

- **The Lolly Sings group.** The original Lolly Sings and its genres. Lolly 1.2.0 ships 26 of them: Sugar, K-pop, Straya, Chiptune, City pop, Funk, Soul, Nu-disco, Hip hop, Trip hop, Reggae, Spanish guitar, Rock, Metal, Country, Blues, Jazz, Swing, Fantasy, Dreamscape, Synthwave, Acid, Electro, Dubstep, Brostep and D&B. Fifteen more have arrived since 1.2.0: Sherbert, Persian, Guzheng, Koto, Kora, Afrobeat, Amapiano, Ska, Punk, R&B, Grunge, Stoner rock, Shoegaze, Lofi house and Botanica.
- **The Lofi group.** Three moods: Warm, Late night and Rainy.

The **[music generator](#the-music-generator)** opens any of these stations as a track you can change, down to the instrument each part plays in each section.

## Every tune is a number

A tune comes from two things: its station and a **seed**, a whole number from 0 to 4,294,967,295. The composer turns the station and the seed into the same tune every time, on every device: the same key and tempo, the same instruments, the same groove and running order, and every note in the same place. It reads no clock, rolls no dice of its own and fetches nothing from the internet, so there is nothing else for a tune to depend on.

That is what makes the music deterministic, and everything below follows from that rule:

- **A shared link plays the same tune.** The player's link button, **Share this tune or moment**, offers two links. **This tune** plays the tune from its start, with a link such as `#/?lofi=sugar.2024`. **This moment** also starts from the bar the tune had reached and shows the visualiser as you were seeing it, with its preset, colours, brand tint and preset cycle, for example `#/?lofi=sugar.2024&ns.at=11&ns.viz=pulse`. Whoever opens either link hears the tune you heard, on the gallery, utilities or assets view the link names. The visualiser look is theirs for that visit only and replaces none of their own settings. Internet radio and catalog tracks share the same way by their ids (`listen=…`), from the second you had reached. A track you uploaded stays on your device, so the card says so instead of offering a link.
- **A saved tune is a few bytes.** **Save to library** keeps the station and the seed as an asset in your library, about 70 bytes, not a recording. Playing the tune later composes the same music again. Lolly Demo and Lolly Start each ship six saved tunes.
- **A music bed renders the same audio.** A saved tune used as a video's music bed is rendered from the seed when the video is made, repeating to fill the video.
- **Next and Previous move between seeds.** Next starts a new tune with a fresh seed. Previous goes back to the seed before, so a tune you skipped past is never lost.

## What stays the same, and what can differ

![The station and the seed go to Lolly's composer, which reads no clock and rolls no dice, and the tune it writes is the same for everyone. Your browser synthesises the sound from that tune, with your own settings for drums, texture and volume applied on top.](/info/diagrams/concepts/tune-from-seed.svg)

**Stays the same for everyone:**

- The notes, their timing and their loudness
- The instruments, the tempo and the key
- The running order: intro, verses, builds, drops and the ending

**Can differ:**

- Your **Drums** and **Texture** settings, applied on top of the tune
- Your volume, your speakers and your headphones
- How an instrument sounds after a Lolly update that improves that instrument
- A percussion part (a shaker, a tambourine, congas) that an update adds to a genre; the other parts keep their notes
- New sounds a genre gains: Dubstep, Brostep and D&B now end each phrase of a drop with the bass talking, which takes over the second half of that bar's bass line, and add bleeps and phaser sweeps around the rest

A Lolly update can change how an instrument is synthesised, to fix a harsh sound or balance a mix. The tune keeps its notes, so a shared link still plays the same piece. New stations are written so that existing tunes do not change, and a genre that gains new parts says so in the list above. Lolly's tests pin known seeds to the tunes they compose, so an update that would rewrite an existing tune by accident is caught before release.

## How a tune is made

Each station leans the composer towards its genre without fixing the outcome.

1. **A style.** The seed picks the instruments (keys, lead, bass and drum kit), how the drums are tuned, the groove, how often the chords change, and how much tape wobble and room the tune gets.
2. **Harmony and melody.** The tune draws two chord progressions in its key, voices each chord close to the last, and writes a short melody and its answer from the key's pentatonic scale, with a chord tone on each strong beat.
3. **A running order.** Instruments enter, take the front and drop out at different moments in each tune, and an intro opens on the beat, the bass, a lead or the whole band. The Lolly Sings dance genres build tension with a roll and a riser, open the drop on a crash, and change instruments between sections, with softer keys in the quiet parts and a second lead and bass in the second drop. The band genres play songs: verses, a pre-chorus that turns into the chorus with a fill round the toms, a solo and a last chorus. Blues plays twelve-bar choruses with the turnaround as its intro, and Jazz and Swing trade four bars with the drummer.
4. **Sound.** Your browser plays the notes with oscillators, filters and noise. The slap bass in Sugar is shaped by frequency modulation, so it can be thumbed, popped, slid and bent. The horns brighten as they get louder, as real horns do, with breath that buzzes at the note's pitch. The plucked strings are modelled as strings: a guitar, a banjo, a harp, an upright bass and the zithers and lutes of the world styles each play a string of the right length that swings in two planes, losing its high overtones faster than its low ones. Each string is plucked at its own point along its length, softer plucks being darker, and a nylon or flatwound string goes mellow sooner than a steel one. An electric guitar is heard through its pickup, the bridge pickup thinner and brighter than the neck pickup, and its distorted parts drive the amp with those strings rather than with a synthesiser's waveform. The band also plays what the composer writes the way people play it, rather than exactly as written. The drummer sits in the style's pocket, with the snare laid back in hip hop and soul and the hats pushed in punk, and plays each hit a few milliseconds off the grid, the hats more than the kick. No two strokes are the same strength, and the hats lean on the beat. The machine styles stay tight, as drum machines are. A guitarist fingers each chord on the fretboard, as a folk player does with open strings ringing or as a jazz player does up the neck, and strums it: down from the low strings on the beat, up across the treble strings in between, faster when harder. A lead guitarist bends some long notes up into pitch and slides into some from the note before. The acoustic drums are worked out from the instruments themselves before a tune starts: a cymbal is dozens of partials ringing at their own pitches, the high ones dying first, with the wash blooming just after the stick strikes; a ride has its bell; hats are two plates, choked shut, and a closed hat cuts off an open one; under every cymbal's ringing partials are its far denser modes, too close together to hear apart, as dozens of narrow bands of noise ringing where the cymbal rings, so a crash is mostly wash and a ride keeps its ping; a drumhead rings at the membrane's own modes, starting sharp when struck hard and falling back into pitch; a tom is struck, not summed: the stick's moment on the head (shorter for a hard stroke, which reaches higher) sets its modes ringing, pulled closer together by the air inside it, with the resonant head a hair apart so the note beats and blooms, and the shell's short rings under it; and a snare's wires buzz for as long as the head moves, and buzz softly in sympathy when the kick or a tom is struck. Each drum is rendered at three strengths with three takes of each, so no two hits in a row are the same. The kit then plays in a room of its own, so a snare rings out and drops off, and sits across the speakers as overhead microphones hear it, hats to the left and the ride to the right. Electronic styles keep their synthesised metal, and each genre has its own hand percussion. Where a game is played (chiptune, Sugar and Sherbert), the console has more to say: a level-up fanfare ends a build, the music shrinks as a breakdown starts and grows back as it ends, a 1-up crowns a drop, and coins ring, echo or come in pairs, brighter and longer the harder they play. Most tunes are signed the way a producer signs a track: a voice says "Lolly" once, at the top, as the moment before the first drop or chorus runs out, or at the end of the outro. The voice is synthesised, not recorded: a glottal pulse and breath through the formants of a vocal tract, moving through the word's sounds, with the dip of the second l that makes it two syllables. How it is said suits the style. It can be whispered or sung on the key in the calm styles, spoken or sung in jazz and soul, shouted by one person or a crowd in a band's. A club tune pitches it up or down, chops it into a stutter, says it like a robot or throws it into the echo. Each tune draws its own speaker, a lower or a higher voice, so no two tags sound quite alike. The texture is a record's: tiny ticks of dust, each a short ring in one groove wall or the other so it sits in stereo, an occasional lower pop, a scratch that ticks once a revolution on some records, and the soft noise of the stylus in the groove, swelling gently as the record turns, rather than a steady hiss.

::: details Where the newer instruments' sounds come from
The instruments of Persian, Guzheng, Koto, Kora and Amapiano follow published acoustics and openly licensed instrument models:

- plucked and struck strings shaped by where they are played;
- bars and drumheads as their measured modes (the balafon's from the Synthesis ToolKit's wood bar);
- breathy flutes and bowed strings through their bodies' resonances;
- the log drum after the MIT-licensed mda DX10's preset of that name.

A Persian dastgah's koron is played a quarter-tone flat, a close approximation of the neutral steps musicians play.

The saxophone and the clarinet are blown, not played from a waveform. Each is a model of the instrument after Perry Cook's in the Synthesis ToolKit: the breath presses on a reed that closes as the pressure across it rises, and the reed drives a bore, a cone for the sax and a cylinder for the clarinet. The cone sounds every harmonic and the cylinder leads with the odd ones in its low register. Blown harder, the reed beats further and the note brightens and buzzes. What you hear is the wave leaving the sax's bell, so none of the cone's harmonics is missing. A tongued sax note reaches its full level in about 30 milliseconds and a held note's pitch wanders by a couple of cents, as a recorded alto's notes do, and a held note gets a jaw vibrato. The brass and the flute start from their instruments' spectra (brass's even spread, the flute's near-pure tone) and get brighter the harder they are played. A note that starts as the one before ends is slurred: the instrument glides into the new pitch without a fresh attack, as a player does without tonguing. Every tune also plays its own drum kit, drawn from its seed and its kind of music: an acoustic bass drum for a band, a feathered one for jazz, a dusty boom for lofi, an 808 or a punchier electronic kick for dance music, with each kit's snare wires, hats, ride, crash (or splash, or china) and toms. A tom struck hard starts as much as 40 per cent sharp and falls back into pitch over a few tenths of a second, with the stick's click and the head's thud in its first 50 milliseconds, as an acoustic kit's recorded toms do. A drum struck harder is brighter as well as louder, and a ghost note is a soft dark tap. A jazz kit has a dark, washy ride with little bell, no china, and small toms tuned high. Jazz and swing keep the hi-hat's foot on two and four under the ride, and their soloists lay back a little behind the beat.

Sherbert was tuned by measuring three Australian future-bass records of the mid-2010s for tempo, groove, stereo width and tonal balance (134 to 145 beats a minute, half-time, chords as wide as the speakers over a mono sub bass); it copies none of their notes or sounds.
:::

## The music generator

**Music generator** (under Utilities, `#/music`, or **Open in the music generator** in the player's controls while a generated station plays) is a track editor for the same stations. One row of controls holds play, shuffle, the genre, the seed, the length, the key, the tempo and **Reset**. Below it are a visualiser and the track itself, laid out like a sequence editor: the sections and chords along the top, a lane for the lead, keys, bass, drums and effects, and every note drawn. Each section is named for its genre: a band's drop is its chorus, its break the solo.

**Make a track** in four steps:

::: steps
- <!--i:eti-sparkles--> **Pick a start** Choose a genre and a seed, or shuffle for both. Set the key and tempo if you like.
- <!--i:eti-layers--> **Change instruments by section** Click a section in a lane, hear the instruments in that section and use one there or all through the track.
- <!--i:eti-sliders--> **Mix the parts** Set each part's level, the effects, the room and the drive.
- <!--i:eti-download--> **Keep the track** Save the tune to your library, or download an M4A or WAV file.
:::

The controls in full:

- **Drums and effects.** The drums track's browser chooses a kit (acoustic, jazz, dusty, hip-hop, electronic or 808) in place of the style's own, and clicking a kit plays two bars of the tune's own drums on that kit. A kit changes how the drums sound, never which notes they play. The effects track's menu chooses how the tune says "Lolly": as the composer placed it, not at all, or one delivery for every tag, placed at the top when the composer placed none. In **Sound and mix**, the effect switches turn off the voices (the shouts, the birds and the tag), the console's sounds, the sparkles or the sweeps.
- **The lead's place.** Each genre decides how far the lead stands out from the band: further back in the calm styles, forward in jazz and swing, where the soloist is the point. Each instrument is first evened out against the others, so a bright synth lead and a quiet flute sit in the same place. An instrument you choose for the lead takes that place too.
- **Length.** A track ends the way a song does. It holds whole tunes, and where the length you chose falls partway through a tune, that tune is cut at the end of a phrase and finished with its own outro and fade. A build always runs into its drop before any cut. The length can come out longer or shorter than the one you chose: a tune that would lose only its last few bars plays to the end, and a later tune that would get fewer than 16 bars before its outro is left out, so the track ends on the song before it rather than on a scrap of the next.
- **Genre.** The genre button shows the genre playing and opens every genre as a card with an icon, A to Z under Chill (the Lofi stations among them), Electronic, Classic and International. Click a card, or move through the cards with the arrow keys and press Enter, to play that genre. Escape closes the picker.
- **Shuffle.** The round button with the crossing arrows, beside play (at the bottom of the screen on a phone), draws a new genre, a new seed and a sound to suit them: some parts keep the composer's instrument and some get another that the style plays, now and then a kind of section gets an instrument of its own, and the mix, the texture, the brightness and the drive move a little either side of the style's own. The tempo goes back to the tune's own and the length stays yours. Turn off **Shuffle genres** at the top of the genre picker to keep the genre and draw only a new seed and sound; the setting is kept with your profile. Hold Shift to keep the genre, the seed and the key, and shuffle only the sound. A playing track plays on in the new one. A shuffle draws your favourite instruments more often and never draws one you blocked.
- **Opening.** The generator opens on a track drawn at random, as **Shuffle** draws one. A link to a track opens that track.
- **Reset** keeps the style and the seed and puts every other setting back to the composer's own: 64 bars, every part automatic, the tune's own key and tempo, every level at its designed level, and nothing muted or soloed.
- **The seed.** Type any seed, step with the arrows, or drag across the number to slide through seeds and watch the tune change.
- **The key.** The key menu moves the whole tune up to six semitones up or down, and lists each key by name. Every pitched part moves together and the drums stay as they are.
- **The tempo.** Each tune plays at its own tempo until you set one: type a number of beats a minute in the tempo field, or drag across the field. The notes stay where they are in the bar, so the tune plays faster or slower and nothing else changes. The arrow next to the tempo field returns to the tune's own tempo. The player always plays a station at the tune's own tempo.
- **Instruments.** Each instrument draws its notes in its own shade of the part's colour. Where a part changes instrument, a line of the new shade runs down the lane, and a chip shows the instrument's family icon and its name. The chip is filled when you chose the instrument and tinted when the composer did. Each lane's head shows the instrument playing at the playhead.
- **The instrument browser.** A track's head (a track's row on a phone) or a section in its lane opens the browser for the lead, the keys or the bass: every instrument as a card, grouped by family (keys, guitars and strings, bells and mallets, winds and brass, voices, world, synths and 8-bit). Click a card to hear two bars of this tune's part on that instrument, in the kind of section chosen at the top, with the other parts turned down. **Use here** plays that instrument in every section of that kind (the bass in every chorus, the lead in every solo), and **Use everywhere** plays it all through the track. **Let the composer choose** goes back to the composer's own. The drums' browser holds the kits, and the snare and cymbals for a kind of section. Nothing else about the tune changes. On a wide screen the browser is a panel beside the track, and on a phone a sheet from the bottom.
- **Favourite and blocked instruments.** The star on a card makes an instrument a favourite, which a shuffle draws more often. The block button keeps an instrument out of shuffles and new tunes: when the composer would play a blocked instrument in a new tune (another seed, style or length, or a shuffle), the generator plays another the style plays in its place and puts that choice in the link, so the link sounds the same for everyone. A link that sets a blocked instrument still plays that instrument. Your favourites and blocks are kept with your profile.
- **Playing.** Click the top row to play from a section, double-click a bar to play from that bar, and press space to play or stop. Zoom in, zoom out or fit the whole track; a wide screen shows more of the track.
- **On a phone.** Play, the time and shuffle sit along the bottom of the screen, the genre button runs across the top, and each track is a row showing the instruments it plays, then its mute and solo buttons. Tap a row to open its instruments. The visualiser shows while something plays, and the full track is below the rows.
- **Sound and mix.** The sliders button opens the levels of each part, the effects (the risers, the console's sounds, the shouts, the birds, the sparkles, phasers and bleeps), the room, the texture, the brightness and the drive. Each is a pill with its name and value written on it: press anywhere along the pill to set the level or drag along the pill, and the arrow keys step the level. The effects lane has its own mute and solo.
- **Render and play.** Play plays the track live, as fast as your device can work it out. **Render and play** works the whole track out first at full quality, with denser cymbals and more oversampling than live playing can afford, while a progress card shows how far it has got and about how long is left. A render can take longer than the track itself, and Cancel or Escape stops the render. The rendered track then plays with the full visualiser, which can go full screen. A track already rendered plays straight away, and a change to the track goes back to live playing until it is rendered again.
- **Keeping a track.** **Save to library** keeps the track as a tune: a few hundred bytes holding everything it was made with (its style and seed, instruments, tempo, key, mix and length), saved at once with nothing to render first. The tune plays in the Neurospicy player beside your own audio and in Assets with a player, and you can pick it as an audiogram's sound or a video's music bed (it loops to fill a longer video), always exactly as you made the track. **Make the track** renders it with progress, scheduling its notes a few seconds ahead as it goes, then **Download** saves an M4A or WAV file. Every setting is in the link, so the link button copies a track that anyone can open, play and change.

Escape closes the instrument browser or a panel, then leaves the generator. Focus goes back to the control that opened the browser.

## What a track records

A track the generator makes says that Lolly composed it, so nobody mistakes it for a recording or for AI output. The Make panel says what each write records before you click.

- **A saved tune.** **Save to library** records who made the tune, as the name in your profile under Settings → Preferences → Your details. With no name in the profile, no maker is recorded. The maker is a statement of who made the track, not a copyright claim. The tune also records that it was made in the music generator, and the Lolly and engine versions that made the track. Its licence starts as **Not recorded**. Open the tune in Assets to see each record in its details: **Source** reads *Made in the music generator*, with **Made by**, **Made with** and **Licence** below. The **Licence** menu sets a licence for your tune (CC0, CC BY, CC BY-SA and their NonCommercial and NoDerivatives forms), and **Not recorded** clears the licence. A tune saved from the Neurospicy player reads *Saved from the Neurospicy player* and records no maker.
- **A download.** A WAV or M4A from **Download** carries [Content Credentials](/info/exporting.html#content-credentials-c2pa) while the **Content Credentials** switch in the Make panel is ticked, which it is by default. The credential's created step says *Made with Lolly's music generator (procedural, no AI)*, then the style and the seed, and the credential carries no name. Its digital source type is algorithmic media: music computed by an algorithm, with no sampled or trained material, which Lolly Verify does not flag as AI. Untick the switch to download the file with no credential. After the download, the line under the track repeats what a reading of the written file found.
- **A tune in another file.** A video or an audio export that plays a saved tune (a video's music bed, an audiogram's sound, a clip on a timeline) records the tune as a source in its Content Credentials: its name, the licence when the tune records one, the SHA-256 of the tune's recipe, the engine version that rendered the tune, and algorithmic media. The maker's name joins that record only while **Use my details to create** is on under Settings → Preferences → Your details, the same switch your name follows everywhere else in an export; with the switch off the name stays on your device and the rest of the record is unchanged. Before you export, a line under **Download** lists the tunes and what the record will name, the maker included only when the name will be written; after the export, the same line reports what a reading of the delivered file found. Untick **C2PA Credentials** to leave the record out. A still image records no tune, because it carries no sound.

## Performance

The Lolly Sings genres play many more synthesised voices at once than the original Lolly Sings and the Lofi stations. On a genre's station, and on a saved tune from one, the player offers **Light visualiser** (the lightning bolt), which shows the simple level bars instead of the full visualiser so the music has the processing time it needs. The setting is on by default on a modest device: one with a touch screen, four processor cores or fewer, or 4 GB of memory or less. You can change it in the station's controls at any time.

## No samples, no recorded voices

Every sound is synthesised by Lolly's own code. A generated tune contains no samples, loops or recordings, and no recorded voice. The vocal sounds are built from the same oscillators and filters as every other instrument: the "hey" shout in Sugar, Reggae and K-pop; the shouts (hey, yeah, woo, oh, ayy, uh, ugh, whoa and yo), which Sherbert calls in series, hip hop and R&B use as ad-libs and punk's and ska's bands call together; and the vocal chops in Sherbert, R&B and lofi house, which sing syllables such as ah, oh, la, da and yeah with no words. The chops come from the formant voice that says the tag: a glottal pulse and breath through the resonances of a vocal tract. Each tune draws its own singer and the handful of syllables she sang, and each note is one slice of that, scooped, slid from the note before, flipped an octave, gated, stuttered or now and then reversed into a phrase, a little different every time. The harder a note is sung, the brighter and more open its vowel. The magpies and the kookaburra are synthesised the same way.

Botanica's sounds of a place are models too, not field recordings. A bird sings syllables: a nearly pure whistle sweeping through each one, with the faint harmonics a bird's voice has, at the pace and in the range of its species. Wind is noise that swells and darkens with each gust, leaves are thousands of tiny contacts a second, running water and drips are bubbles ringing at their resonant pitch, rain is drops striking and landing in water, and crickets, cicadas and bees each sing, buzz or hum at their own rate. Each was measured against field recordings released into the public domain, and each is drawn from the tune's seed, so a tune's birds sing the same song every time.
