# Generated music

The Neurospicy player makes its own music. Its generated stations compose every tune as they play. Nothing is recorded or sampled: each tune is written by Lolly's composer and played by instruments synthesised in your browser.

- **The Lolly Sings group.** The original Lolly Sings and its genres. Lolly 1.2.0 ships 26 of them: Sugar, K-pop, Straya, Chiptune, City pop, Funk, Soul, Nu-disco, Hip hop, Trip hop, Reggae, Spanish guitar, Rock, Metal, Country, Blues, Jazz, Swing, Fantasy, Dreamscape, Synthwave, Acid, Electro, Dubstep, Brostep and D&B. Twenty-eight more have arrived since 1.2.0: Sherbert, Persian, Guzheng, Koto, Kora, Afrobeat, Amapiano, Ska, Punk, R&B, Grunge, Stoner rock, Shoegaze, Lofi house, Salsa, Surf rock, Yacht rock, Botanica, Oompa, Mandopop, C-Pop, Breakbeat, Funky house, UK garage, Private school piano, Noosacore, Arnhem Land and Jungle.
- **The Lofi group.** Three moods: Warm, Late night and Rainy.

The **[music generator](#the-music-generator)** opens any of these stations as a track you can change, down to the instrument each part plays in each section.

## Every tune is a number

A tune comes from two things: its station and a **seed**, a whole number from 0 to 4,294,967,295. The composer turns the station and the seed into the same tune every time, on every device: the same key and tempo, the same instruments, the same groove and running order, and every note in the same place. It reads no clock, rolls no dice of its own and fetches nothing from the internet, so there is nothing else for a tune to depend on.

That is what makes the music deterministic, and everything below follows from that rule:

- **A shared link plays the same tune.** The player's link button, **Share this tune or moment**, offers two links. **This tune** plays the tune from its start, with a link such as `#/?lofi=sugar.2024`. **This moment** also starts from the bar the tune had reached and shows the visualiser as you were seeing it, with its preset, colours, brand tint and preset cycle, for example `#/?lofi=sugar.2024&ns.at=11&ns.viz=pulse`. Whoever opens either link hears the tune you heard, on the gallery, utilities or assets view the link names. The visualiser look is theirs for that visit only and replaces none of their own settings. Internet radio and catalog tracks share the same way by their ids (`listen=…`), from the second you had reached. A track you uploaded stays on your device, so the card says so instead of offering a link.
- **A saved tune is a few bytes.** **Save to library** keeps the station and the seed as an asset in your library, about 70 bytes, not a recording. Playing the tune later composes the same music again. Lolly Demo and Lolly Start each ship six saved tunes.
- **A music bed renders the same audio.** A saved tune used as a video's music bed is rendered from the seed when the video is made, repeating to fill the video.
- **Next and Previous move between seeds.** Next starts a new tune with a fresh seed. Previous goes back to the seed before, so a tune you skipped past is never lost.

## What stays the same, and what can differ

![The station and the seed go to Lolly's composer, which reads no clock and rolls no dice, and the tune it writes is the same for everyone. Your browser synthesises the sound from that tune, with your own settings for drums, texture and volume applied on top.](/info/diagrams/concepts/tune-from-seed.svg)

**Stays the same for everyone:**

- The notes, their timing and their loudness
- The instruments, the tempo and the key
- The running order: intro, verses, builds, drops and the ending

**Can differ:**

- Your **Drums** and **Texture** settings, applied on top of the tune
- Your volume, your speakers and your headphones
- How an instrument sounds after a Lolly update that improves that instrument
- A percussion part (a shaker, a tambourine, congas) that an update adds to a genre; the other parts keep their notes
- New sounds a genre gains: Dubstep, Brostep and D&B now end each phrase of a drop with the bass talking, which takes over the second half of that bar's bass line, and add bleeps and phaser sweeps around the rest
- The bass in the electronic genres (November 2026): Dubstep, Brostep, D&B, Electro, Nu-disco and Lofi house play their bass as a stack, so every tune of theirs sounds different from before. Dubstep and Brostep drops now trade their bass between characters, and the croak calls over the bass in some Dubstep, Brostep, D&B and Sugar tunes instead of playing alone, so those tunes' bass notes changed too (every Dubstep and Brostep tune, about four in ten D&B tunes and about six in ten Sugar tunes)
- D&B's drums (November 2026): through every drop the kick and the snare are edited bar by bar (a kick moved or added, ghost notes and drags into the backbeat, half a bar re-cut, a turnaround to end each four bars and a fill into each eight), and a top loop, a drummer's break chopped and played quietly, sits over them. So every D&B tune's drops sound different from before; the rest of each tune is as it was
- Chopped breaks (November 2026): every Breakbeat tune, about one D&B tune in three, about half of the Hip hop tunes and about three Trip hop tunes in four now play a chopped break: a drummer's two bars recorded once per tune through a model of an old sampler, cut into slices and played back in a new order. In Breakbeat, Hip hop and Trip hop the break takes the place of the kit's drums; in D&B it takes the place of the top loop, over the kit. Those tunes' drums sound different from before, and the rest of each tune keeps its notes. A Jungle tune's drums are its chopped break from the start
- The console's FM effects (November 2026): Sugar, Chiptune and Sherbert tunes gain spins, shots and a moment before each drop, and some K-pop and Synthwave tunes a moment before their last chorus or drop, all drawn on their own, so the rest of each tune keeps its notes. Where a moment or a spin plays, the console sound it replaces (a level-up, a power-up, a coin or a jump at the end of that phrase) no longer plays
- The soft lead is retired (November 2026). It was a simple synth line, and the modelled flute, which breathes and phrases like a player, now does the same job better. In most genres its share of the choices went to the flute. City pop split its share between the flute and a trumpet, and Dreamscape between the flute and bells. Funk, Soul and Reggae gave theirs to a trumpet, Synthwave, Electro, Brostep and UK garage to bells, Amapiano to vibes, Shoegaze to a viola and Trip hop to a cello. Between one tune in twenty (Lofi house) and about four in ten (Funk and Shoegaze) now play a different lead. In the genres that change instruments between sections, most tunes changed somewhere, because their second leads and doublings were drawn again. A link or saved tune that chose the soft lead plays the flute

A Lolly update can change how an instrument is synthesised, to fix a harsh sound or balance a mix. The tune keeps its notes, so a shared link still plays the same piece. New stations are written so that existing tunes do not change, and a genre that gains new parts says so in the list above. Lolly's tests pin known seeds to the tunes they compose, so an update that would rewrite an existing tune by accident is caught before release.

## How a tune is made

Each station leans the composer towards its genre without fixing the outcome.

1. **A style.** The seed picks the instruments (keys, lead, bass and drum kit), how the drums are tuned, the groove, how often the chords change, and how much tape wobble and room the tune gets.
2. **Harmony and melody.** The tune draws two chord progressions in its key, voices each chord close to the last, and writes a short melody and its answer from the key's pentatonic scale, with a chord tone on each strong beat.
3. **A running order.** Instruments enter, take the front and drop out at different moments in each tune, and an intro opens on the beat, the bass, a lead or the whole band. The Lolly Sings dance genres build tension with a roll and a riser, open the drop on a crash, and change instruments between sections, with softer keys in the quiet parts and a second lead and bass in the second drop. The band genres play songs: verses, a pre-chorus that turns into the chorus with a fill round the toms, a solo and a last chorus. Blues plays twelve-bar choruses with the turnaround as its intro, and Jazz and Swing trade four bars with the drummer.
4. **Sound.** Your browser plays the notes with oscillators, filters and noise. The slap bass in Sugar is shaped by frequency modulation, so it can be thumbed, popped, slid and bent. The horns brighten as they get louder, as real horns do, with breath that buzzes at the note's pitch. The plucked strings are modelled as strings: a guitar, a banjo, a harp, an upright bass and the zithers and lutes of the world styles each play a string of the right length that swings in two planes, losing its high overtones faster than its low ones. Each string is plucked at its own point along its length, softer plucks being darker, and a nylon or flatwound string goes mellow sooner than a steel one. An electric guitar is heard through its pickup, the bridge pickup thinner and brighter than the neck pickup, and its distorted parts drive the amp with those strings rather than with a synthesiser's waveform. The steel-string acoustic is heard through its body, measured from recordings of real flat-tops: the air in the box near 110 Hz and the braced top's and back's resonances ring under every pluck, its high partials fade much sooner than an electric's, and the open strings ring in sympathy. The noises of playing are there at the levels measured in recorded flatpicked phrases: the pick's click and scrape, the frets ticking as the fingers press and let go, fingers rubbing the strings between notes and on a shift up the neck, a squeak on a slide along a wound string, and now and then a fret buzz on a hard stroke. As the lead, the guitarist decorates the tune as a flatpicker does: slides, hammer-ons and pull-offs, quick runs up or down the key's scale into a note, the chord strummed under a held note, double stops and muted strings raked into a note. About half the guitarists are percussive players, who knock the top on the backbeat of their strumming and on the beats the lead rests over. Strummed, it plays as the recorded strummers do: the hand keeps moving, down on the beats and up between, about two strokes a beat; each stroke sweeps the strings in about 30 milliseconds; a held chord is strummed through in the tune's own pattern (a boom-chick in country and Straya, sixteenths in the slow styles); the chord rings into the next stroke, which takes each string over; a percussive player chokes some backbeats into a muted chuck; and as the lead, about a quarter of the bars are strummed passages between the picked phrases. The band also plays what the composer writes the way people play it, rather than exactly as written. The drummer sits in the style's pocket, with the snare laid back in hip hop and soul and the hats pushed in punk, and plays each hit a few milliseconds off the grid, the hats more than the kick. No two strokes are the same strength, and the hats lean on the beat. The machine styles stay tight, as drum machines are. A guitarist fingers each chord on the fretboard, as a folk player does with open strings ringing or as a jazz player does up the neck, and strums it: down from the low strings on the beat, up across the treble strings in between, faster when harder. A lead guitarist bends some long notes up into pitch and slides into some from the note before. The acoustic drums are worked out from the instruments themselves before a tune starts: a cymbal is dozens of partials ringing at their own pitches, the high ones dying first, with the wash blooming just after the stick strikes; a ride has its bell; hats are two plates, choked shut, and a closed hat cuts off an open one; under every cymbal's ringing partials are its far denser modes, too close together to hear apart, as dozens of narrow bands of noise ringing where the cymbal rings, so a crash is mostly wash and a ride keeps its ping; a drumhead rings at the membrane's own modes, starting sharp when struck hard and falling back into pitch; a tom is struck, not summed: the stick's moment on the head (shorter for a hard stroke, which reaches higher) sets its modes ringing, pulled closer together by the air inside it, with the resonant head a hair apart so the note beats and blooms, and the shell's short rings under it; and a snare's wires buzz for as long as the head moves, and buzz softly in sympathy when the kick or a tom is struck. Each drum is rendered at three strengths with three takes of each, so no two hits in a row are the same. The kit then plays in a room of its own, so a snare rings out and drops off, and sits across the speakers as overhead microphones hear it, hats to the left and the ride to the right. Electronic styles keep their synthesised metal, and each genre has its own hand percussion. Where a game is played (chiptune, Sugar and Sherbert), the console has more to say: the music shrinks as a breakdown starts and grows back as it ends, a 1-up crowns a drop, and coins ring, echo or come in pairs, brighter and longer the harder they play. A second console joins in, in the style of the 16-bit machines' FM sound chip: four operators with feedback, pitches stepped once a video frame on the chip's own frequency grid, square waves and noise from the second chip beside them, and the gritty 9-bit converter and soft output filter of the early consoles. Each drop has one moment before it, a different one each time: a power-up climbing through the drop's chord, a warp diving three octaves across the speakers, a spin that charges up and fires a shot as the drop hits, a short start-of-game fanfare in two voices, or the tape stopping under the music for the last beat. A spin revs up through its gears into some builds, and a few laser shots answer the tune through each drop, off the beat while the lead holds a note, their echoes answering from one side and then the other and darker each time: a short shot, a pair, a long buzzing beam, or a charged shot after a short spin. These are Lolly's own sounds in that style, not copies of any game's. Sherbert's builds mostly end on its chopped stutter, a bird or the tag, so its moments are rarer, and its shots go in the breakdown's spaces. K-pop and Synthwave borrow a moment before their last chorus or drop in about one tune in four. Most tunes are signed the way a producer signs a track: a voice says "Lolly" once, at the top, as the moment before the first drop or chorus runs out, or at the end of the outro. The voice is synthesised, not recorded: a glottal pulse and breath through the formants of a vocal tract, moving through the word's sounds, with the dip of the second l that makes it two syllables. How it is said suits the style. It can be whispered or sung on the key in the calm styles, spoken or sung in jazz and soul, shouted by one person or a crowd in a band's. A club tune pitches it up or down, chops it into a stutter, says it like a robot or throws it into the echo. Each tune draws its own speaker, a lower or a higher voice, so no two tags sound quite alike. The texture is a record's: tiny ticks of dust, each a short ring in one groove wall or the other so it sits in stereo, an occasional lower pop, a scratch that ticks once a revolution on some records, and the soft noise of the stylus in the groove, swelling gently as the record turns, rather than a steady hiss.

::: details Where the newer instruments' sounds come from
The instruments of Persian, Guzheng, Koto, Kora and Amapiano follow published acoustics and openly licensed instrument models:

- plucked and struck strings shaped by where they are played;
- bars and drumheads as their measured modes (the balafon's from the Synthesis ToolKit's wood bar);
- breathy flutes and bowed strings through their bodies' resonances;
- the log drum after the MIT-licensed mda DX10's preset of that name.

A Persian dastgah's koron is played a quarter-tone flat, a close approximation of the neutral steps musicians play.

The saxophone and the clarinet are blown, not played from a waveform. Each is a model of the instrument after Perry Cook's in the Synthesis ToolKit: the breath presses on a reed that closes as the pressure across it rises, and the reed drives a bore, a cone for the sax and a cylinder for the clarinet. The cone sounds every harmonic and the cylinder leads with the odd ones in its low register. Blown harder, the reed beats further and the note brightens and buzzes. What you hear is the wave leaving the sax's bell, so none of the cone's harmonics is missing. The sax's breath is heard in its tone, its body is fitted to recorded altos and tenors, and it is louder and brighter the harder it is blown; a tongued note speaks in about 25 milliseconds blown hard and 70 softly, a held note's pitch wanders by a couple of cents, and its vibrato comes in after a moment and grows, as recorded players' does. A saxophonist in Yacht rock growls a few loud held notes and plays soft low ones in the breathy subtone. The brass and the flute start from their instruments' spectra (brass's even spread, the flute's near-pure tone) and get brighter the harder they are played. A note that starts as the one before ends is slurred: the instrument glides into the new pitch without a fresh attack, as a player does without tonguing. Every tune also plays its own drum kit, drawn from its seed and its kind of music: an acoustic bass drum for a band, a feathered one for jazz, a dusty boom for lofi, an 808 or a punchier electronic kick for dance music, with each kit's snare wires, hats, ride, crash (or splash, or china) and toms. A tom struck hard starts as much as 40 per cent sharp and falls back into pitch over a few tenths of a second, with the stick's click and the head's thud in its first 50 milliseconds, as an acoustic kit's recorded toms do. A drum struck harder is brighter as well as louder, and a ghost note is a soft dark tap. A jazz kit has a dark, washy ride with little bell, no china, and small toms tuned high. Jazz and swing keep the hi-hat's foot on two and four under the ride, and their soloists lay back a little behind the beat.

The spring reverb in Surf rock is a model of a real tank, not a reverb preset: two springs that carry high frequencies more slowly than low ones, so each echo arrives as a chirp, its low end first and its top a few tens of milliseconds later, which is the drip after a picked note. Its echo times, its decay at each frequency and its tone were measured from a recorded two-spring tank, and its chirp from a recorded tank's impulse response; the surf amp's clean tone was fitted to a single-coil guitar recorded through a twin-speaker combo, and the tremolo picking's pace to recorded surf instrumentals. None of those recordings is in Lolly, only the numbers.

The Rhodes is a model of the instrument too: a struck tine ringing in front of an electromagnetic pickup, whose pull falls off with distance, so a soft note is nearly a pure tone and a hard one barks with strong low harmonics; low notes are rich and the top of the keyboard nearly a sine, the harmonics die faster than the fundamental, and the tine's bell rings for a moment as the hammer strikes. Its harmonics, decays, bell and attack were measured from recorded Rhodes notes. Surf rock's combo organ is built as a transistor organ is, square waves divided down from twelve top-octave oscillators through its tabs, with the vibrato and the instant keying measured from two recorded combo organs.

The viola, the cello and the double bass are bowed as the fiddle is: the hair grips the string until the string's own pull tears it free, the string slips back and is caught again, and the note is heard through the instrument's body. Each body was fitted to recorded notes of its own instrument. The air in the viola's box rings near 235 hertz, the cello's near 100 and the double bass's near 65, with the wood's first modes a little above, so a low note keeps only as much of its fundamental as the box near it lets through. A violist's stroke speaks in about a tenth of a second and a cellist's in about a fifth, as recorded players' strokes do. A violist sings with a wide vibrato on nearly every held note, a cellist with a narrower one, and a bassist hardly at all. Chosen as the lead, the cello plays the line an octave down and the double bass two octaves down, in their own singing registers. The string section plays the keys' chords: each chord tone goes to the part whose range it sits in (basses, cellos, violas or violins), and each part is three players a few cents and a few milliseconds apart, each with a vibrato of their own, so a held chord is as wide as a recorded section's. The upright bass is the same double bass plucked: its string rings through the same body, its low harmonics outlast its high ones as a recorded bass's do, and the finger knocks the body as it lets go.

Sherbert was tuned by measuring three Australian future-bass records of the mid-2010s for tempo, groove, stereo width and tonal balance (134 to 145 beats a minute, half-time, chords as wide as the speakers over a mono sub bass); it copies none of their notes or sounds.

Sherbert's chords were rebuilt after listeners asked for more complexity, and measured against seven openly licensed future-bass records (under CC BY 4.0, CC BY 3.0 and CC0, from the Internet Archive) and six CC0 chord loops from Freesound, for measurement only: none of their notes or sounds is used. Each chord note is a stack of seven saws (eleven when a tune is rendered), tuned apart on the uneven curve of the Roland JP-8000's supersaw and spread across the speakers, with a thinner stack an octave up, a short bright pluck on the attack, a breath of high noise on top and a dark body an octave under the lowest note. The chords share one chain, as a producer's chord bus does: a high-pass that leaves the low end to the 808, a three-band compressor mixed under the dry chords (the way producers use OTT, which brings up the detail between the hits), a soft clip, a chorus, a short room and a pump that the kick and, in a drop, every beat drive, so the room ducks with the chords. Against the loops, the chords' level now moves 13 dB within a bar (the loops 15, the old chords 9), the low mids are kept in the middle and the top spread wide as the loops' are, the attack stands about 7 dB over the chord a tenth of a second later (the loops about 5, the old chords 3), and each harmonic is spread about 20 cents wide (the loops about 21, the old chords 13). A drop's chords change from bar to bar: a third rhythm in every third bar, held chords gated in three-three-two or chopped in sixteenths, wubs at triplet and dotted rates, a stutter, a gated chord or a triplet run into each fourth bar, and a dive, a gap or a swell to end each eight-bar phrase. Some builds swell into the drop instead of breathing before the first chord. Sherbert's supersaw tunes play the same layered chord; the supersaw in Sugar, K-pop and the other styles is unchanged.

The bass stacks of Dubstep, Brostep, D&B, Electro, Nu-disco and Lofi house were tuned by measuring the drops of openly licensed records in those genres and in house and UK garage (from the Internet Archive and Jamendo, under Creative Commons licences or the artist's public domain mark, with CC0 bass loops from Freesound), for measurement only: none of their notes or sounds is used. In the recent ones the low end is mono: below 120 Hz the difference between the two channels is 30 to 50 dB under the middle, and it stays mono up to about 200 Hz before spreading. Above that the bass's middle is wide in Dubstep and D&B (the channels' correlation 0.6 to 0.8 from 150 Hz to 2 kHz) and narrower in house and garage (about 0.9 to 0.97). Between 30 and 100 Hz there is 7 to 10 dB more energy than between 150 Hz and 2 kHz in Dubstep and D&B. The music dips 7 to 16 dB under each kick and recovers in about a quarter of a second, and the wobbles move at two, three or four swings a beat, locked to the tempo. The bass loops made to sit over a sub carry little under 150 Hz and are wide; the full basses carry most of their energy under 150 Hz. Lolly's basses had been one channel all the way up. Now the low end measures as the records do (the side 35 to 45 dB down below 120 Hz), the middle spreads (correlation 0.69 to 0.86 in Dubstep, Brostep and D&B, about 0.95 in the house styles), and the low end sits about 8 dB over the middle in Dubstep and D&B, with each style's bass as loud against the rest of the mix as it was, within half a decibel. Sugar keeps its slap bass on its own: the croak, which played alone in some of its verses until now, carried only 40 per cent of its energy under 150 Hz where the slap and the full bass loops carry 70 to 90, so it calls over the slap instead.

Salsa's grand piano and its Latin percussion are models too, worked out note by note and measured against recordings of real instruments. Nothing recorded is shipped. The piano was fitted to two recorded grand pianos at several strengths: the hammer reaching further up the string the harder it strikes, the stiff string's sharpened partials, the three strings of a note beating and dying away at two rates, the soundboard's knock and the damper. The congas, bongos, timbales, bells, clave and güiro were fitted to recorded single strokes: each drumhead's modes and how long they ring, the hand's slap, the stick on the shell, the güiro's uneven ridges and the stick rubbing between them, rung through the gourd, and the maracas' seeds striking their shells. The mix was balanced against four recorded salsa bands. Only the style's traits were taken from them: no song, arrangement or artist is copied.

Oompa's band is blown, not sampled. The tuba, the flugelhorn, the baritone and the tenor horn are the trumpet's lip model with bores of their own: the lips a mass on a spring that the breath pushes open, driving a bore whose bell lets its harmonics go from its own cutoff, a few hundred hertz on a tuba and about a kilohertz on a flugelhorn. Each was fitted to recordings of the real instrument at several strengths: the tuba to the Philharmonia's tuba notes, F1 to B♭2, pianissimo to fortissimo, where the harmonics come within 6.4 dB of the recordings on average over the first ten (the trombone it would otherwise borrow was 18.3 dB away); the flugelhorn to two recorded players (9.6 dB, the trumpet 18.0); and the baritone to a recorded euphonium (6.7 dB, the trombone 8.8). No tenor horn was found under a licence that allows measuring, so it is the baritone's bore with a smaller bell. A big bore's loop takes a third of a second to build by itself, far slower than a player, so the band's notes speak as recorded players' do, a soft tuba note in about a fifth of a second and a loud one in a few hundredths. The clarinet was checked against recorded clarinets too and its upper register refitted: above the break its second harmonic now comes up as a real clarinet's does, within 4.8 dB of the University of Iowa's E♭ clarinet where the old model was 7.0 dB away. Every player has their own tuning and their own place against the beat, the whole band drifts a little ahead and behind as one, every tongued note starts with the air the tongue lets go, a player who has played a while breathes in the next rest, and the band plays in a hall whose sound dies away in 0.8 to 0.9 seconds below a kilohertz. Measured the same way as five recorded Bavarian and Austrian bands (field recordings and a rehearsal, under Creative Commons licences or the public domain mark, measurement only), the oom sounds 24 ms after the beat (the recorded bands 19 to 26 ms), the after-beat half a beat after it (0.53 to 0.65), the players' partials spread 7 cents (3 to 14) and the mix's octave bands sit within about 1.5 dB of the recorded bands' middle from 125 Hz to 8 kHz.

The erhu and the dizi of Guzheng, Mandopop and C-Pop are modelled and fitted to recordings too (openly licensed recordings, measurement only: nothing recorded is shipped). The erhu is the fiddle's bowed string heard through a python skin over a small box. The skin gives it a weak low fundamental, a nasal peak between 1.6 and 1.9 kHz and a bright edge above 3 kHz. Without a fingerboard a stopped string ends on the finger's soft pad, which rounds each wave, while the two open strings ring from the nut. The bow's hair, passing between the two strings, damps the string it is not playing. Fitted to the ErhuPT dataset, four Freesound recordings and the FolkMusic dataset's erhu clips, its held harmonics come within 3.2 dB of the recordings on average, register by register, and over the same phrases its long-term spectrum is 4.6 to 9 dB from each recording, where the voice it replaced was 10 to 18 dB away. A stroke speaks in about 20 milliseconds loud and 50 soft, as recorded strokes do, and the player sings a slow, wide vibrato on most long notes, slides along the string between notes and into the notes the tune bends, and lets some held notes fall off into a rest. The dizi is the jet flute with its dimo, the thin membrane over a hole near the mouth hole: driven by the bore, the membrane buzzes and adds the odd harmonics a recorded dizi carries only 11 to 16 dB under the whole, where a Western flute's fall much further. Fitted to the FolkMusic dataset's dizi and to Freesound notes and phrases, its harmonics come within 4.3 dB of the recordings (the flute alone is 14.1 dB away). Its player flicks grace notes from the hole above, slides off a hole into a note, turns round a note, flutter-tongues a long, loud note at about 18 a second and falls off a note before a breath.

Breakbeat, Funky house, UK garage and Private school piano were tuned by measuring 27 openly licensed records from the Internet Archive in those styles, in amapiano and in house (under Creative Commons licences, some of them non-commercial or no-derivatives, for measurement only: none of their notes or sounds is used). Each was measured over 16 bars of its fullest section. The breakbeat records loop a one- or two-bar break and edit the loop at the end of a phrase: in most of them no bar's drums differ from the bar before by much (a cosine distance of 0.15 between the bars' onsets), and at most a quarter of the bars do. Their sixteenths are nearly straight (the off-beat sixteenth at 0.49 to 0.61 of the eighth). Funky house swings a little (0.49 to 0.62) and repeats its bars, UK garage swings hardest (0.50 to 0.65, the speed garage records 0.65), and a true two-step keeps its kick off beat three. The amapiano records sit at 111 to 117 beats a minute and swing 0.50 to 0.67. Under 150 Hz the mix sits 2.3 to 6.6 dB under the whole in breakbeat, 0.7 to 6.1 in funky house, 1.8 to 3.9 in UK garage and 2.7 to 7.9 in amapiano, and the music dips 6 to 14 dB under each kick. Lolly's breaks first changed nearly every bar (0.8 to 1.0 of the bars, by the same measure), so they now loop their first bar and turn at the end of each eight, with a drag on the snare to end each four: 0.14 to 0.21 of the bars change. The garage drums are a programmed loop of two bars that a fill turns every eight, as the records' are. D&B's drums used to repeat: alone, they changed in none to 0.07 of a drop's bars, where the two D&B records measured change 0.27 and 0.80, their kick and snare moving around the two-step. Its drops now edit the kick and the snare bar by bar around the same two-step, with a turnaround every four bars and a fill every eight: 0.43 to 0.79 of the bars change, with 6 to 9 different bars in 16 (the records 7 and 9).

The chopped breaks and Jungle were fitted to openly licensed recordings, for measurement only: none of their notes or sounds is used, and none of the famous breaks was measured or copied. Twelve takes of drummers playing funk in a room (Freesound, CC0 and CC BY) set the drummer: their sixteenths are nearly straight (0.50 of the eighth, the hats a little more swung), the backbeat sits on the beat (a median of a third of a millisecond late), and the ghost notes come about two a bar, 17 dB under the backbeat. The modelled kit, recorded the same way, first had no room to speak of (a snare's tail fell 20 dB in 22 ms where the takes' fall in about 80) and a hole between 500 Hz and 2 kHz. Its room, its desk and its snare were set until its octave bands sit within about 2 dB of the takes' between 63 Hz and 8 kHz, and its snare's tail falls 20 dB in 75 to 140 ms. Nine sampler-era breaks, four of them made on an SP-1200, set the sampler: a steep filter near 11 kHz, almost nothing above 13 kHz (57 dB down), and the record's noise about 35 dB under the hits. Lolly's twelve-bit samplers measure 57 dB down above 13 kHz with their noise 33 to 37 dB under. Eight jungle records' drops sit at 170 to 193 beats a minute with the sub 3.5 LU under the breaks, K-weighted (from 11 under to level); Lolly's jungle drops sit 1.8 to 3.7 under. The records loop their breaks between edits, a bar in eight to a bar in two unlike the bars before; Lolly's first drop edits about a bar in three and its second about a bar in two. The other styles' breaks were set against their records the same way: the sub 1 to 2 LU under the breaks in breakbeat, level with them in hip-hop, and 2 to 4 under in trip-hop.

## The music generator

**Music generator** (under Utilities, `#/music`, or **Open in the music generator** in the player's controls while a generated station plays) is a track editor for the same stations. One row of controls holds play, the seed, shuffle, the genre lock, the genre, undo and redo, **Render**, **Save** and the link; the **Tune** section below it holds the length, the key, the tempo and **Reset**, and **Mix** the levels and the ear candy. Below them are a visualiser and the track itself, laid out like a sequence editor: the sections and chords along the top, a lane for each part, and every note drawn. Each section is named for its genre: a band's drop is its chorus, its break the solo.

The lanes are named for the part each one plays in the tune: **Melody** (the tune itself), **Chords** (guitars, strings, harps and saw chords as often as keys), **Bass**, **Drums** and **Ear candy** (the sounds laid over the tune: birds, waves, shouts, the Lolly tag, a games console, sparkles and sweeps). A link still uses their older ids (`lead`, `keys`, `bass`, `drums` and `fx`), so every link made before the names changed plays as it did.

**Make a track** in four steps:

::: steps
- <!--i:eti-sparkles--> **Pick a start** Choose a genre and a seed, or shuffle for both. Set the key and tempo if you like.
- <!--i:eti-layers--> **Change instruments by section** Click a section in a lane, hear the instruments in that section and use one there or all through the track.
- <!--i:eti-sliders--> **Mix the parts** Set each part's level, the effects, the room and the drive.
- <!--i:eti-download--> **Keep the track** Save the tune to your library, or download an M4A or WAV file.
:::

The controls in full:

- **Drums and ear candy.** The drums track's browser chooses a kit (acoustic, jazz, dusty, hip-hop, electronic or 808) in place of the genre's own, which the lane head shows as **Auto** and the kit's name (**Auto · Jazz kit**, **Auto · Straya kit**), and clicking a kit plays two bars of the tune's own drums on that kit. A kit changes how the drums sound, never which notes they play. The Ear candy track's menu chooses how the tune says "Lolly": **Auto** (as the composer placed the tag, with the delivery it chose), not at all, or one delivery for every tag, placed at the top when the composer placed none. In **Mix**, the ear candy switches turn off the voices (the shouts, the birds and the tag), the console's sounds (its FM spins, shots and moments with them), the sparkles, the sweeps, the Aussie birds or the waves. The last two work the other way in a genre without its own: **Aussie birds** sprinkles the magpie and the kookaburra in, and **Waves** lays the sea under the track, the shore's roar and waves breaking at irregular gaps, in front where the music is quiet and under it where the music is full. Noosacore has both on, and in Noosacore **Waves** also turns the she-oaks' breeze on and off. The link carries each choice.
- **Ear candy moments.** Each sound on the Ear candy lane shows as a small icon where it comes in: a bird, the sea's waves, a games console, a riser or a sweep, a shout or the Lolly tag, sparkles, or the sounds of a place. A sound that plays bar after bar is one icon at its start. Point at an icon, or move to one with the keyboard, to see its name. Press one while the track is stopped to hear that moment on its own, with every other part turned off; press one while the track plays and the music carries on from that moment's bar. The icons are one stop for Tab, and the arrow keys move from one moment to the next through the whole track, scrolling the track as they go.
- **The melody's place.** Each genre decides how far the melody stands out from the band: further back in the calm styles, forward in jazz and swing, where the soloist is the point. Each instrument is first evened out against the others, so a bright synth lead and a quiet flute sit in the same place. An instrument you choose for the melody takes that place too.
- **Length.** A track ends the way a song does. It holds whole tunes, and where the length you chose falls partway through a tune, that tune is cut at the end of a phrase and finished with its own outro and fade. A build always runs into its drop before any cut. The length can come out longer or shorter than the one you chose: a tune that would lose only its last few bars plays to the end, and a later tune that would get fewer than 16 bars before its outro is left out, so the track ends on the song before it rather than on a scrap of the next.
- **Genre.** The genre button shows the genre playing: its icon, its name and under the name a line of what you are hearing (Straya's reads "Didgeridoo, banjo and bush birds"), on the genre's own colours from your design system: the colours of its card in the picker, much stronger, so whoever opens a shared link can tell at once what plays. On a light theme a deep colour carries light text; the text keeps at least 4.5:1 contrast on every colour, in light and dark. When the genre changes, by a shuffle, the picker, an undo or a link, the button lights up for a moment; it changes without moving when you prefer reduced motion, in your system's settings or in Lolly's. Clicking the button opens every genre as a card with an icon, each card in its own wash of your design system's colours, A to Z under Chill (the Lofi stations among them), Rock & Roots, Soul & Groove, Club, Bass & Synth and World. A **Recent** row at the top holds the last six genres you played, newest first; it follows your profile like your instrument preferences, and shows once a genre has played. Type in the search field to narrow the cards to the titles with a word that starts with what you typed, so "house" finds Lofi house and Funky house; clear the field to see the groups again. Click a card, or move through the cards with the arrow keys and press Enter, to play that genre. Escape clears the search, then closes the picker.
- **Shuffle.** The round button with the crossing arrows, beside play (at the bottom of the screen on a phone), draws a new genre, a new seed and a sound to suit them: some parts keep the composer's instrument and some get another that the style plays, now and then a kind of section gets an instrument of its own, and the mix, the texture, the brightness and the drive move a little either side of the style's own. The tempo goes back to the tune's own and the length stays yours. The padlock between shuffle and the genre button (next to the genre button on a phone) says whether shuffle may change the genre: open, shuffle draws a new genre; closed, shuffle keeps the genre and draws only a new seed and sound. The line under the track says which, and the setting is kept with your profile. A closed padlock also goes into the link (`lock=genre`), so whoever opens a shared link shuffles within the same genre until they open the padlock. Hold Shift to keep the genre, the seed and the key, and shuffle only the sound. A playing track plays on in the new one. A shuffle draws your favourite instruments more often and never draws one you blocked.
- **Opening.** The generator opens on a track drawn at random, as **Shuffle** draws one. A link to a track opens that track.
- **Reset** keeps the style and the seed and puts every other setting back to the composer's own: 64 bars, every part automatic, the tune's own key and tempo, every level at its designed level, and nothing muted or soloed.
- **The seed.** Type any seed, step with the arrows, or drag across the number to slide through seeds and watch the tune change.
- **The key.** The key menu moves the whole tune up to six semitones up or down, and lists each key by name. Every pitched part moves together and the drums stay as they are.
- **The tempo.** Each tune plays at its own tempo until you set one: type a number of beats a minute in the tempo field, or drag across the field. The notes stay where they are in the bar, so the tune plays faster or slower and nothing else changes. The arrow next to the tempo field returns to the tune's own tempo. The player always plays a station at the tune's own tempo.
- **Instruments.** Each instrument draws its notes in its own shade of the part's colour. Where a part changes instrument, a line of the new shade runs down the lane, and a chip shows the instrument's family icon and its name. The chip is filled when you chose the instrument and tinted when the composer did. Each lane's head shows the instrument playing at the playhead: its name when you chose it, and **Auto** with its name when the composer did (**Auto · Yidaki**).
- **The instrument browser.** A track's head (a track's row on a phone) or a section in its lane opens the browser for the melody, the chords or the bass: every instrument as a card, grouped by family (keys, guitars and strings, bells and mallets, winds and brass, voices, world, synths and 8-bit). Click a card to hear two bars of this tune's part on that instrument, in the kind of section chosen at the top, with the other parts turned down. **Use here** plays that instrument in every section of that kind (the bass in every chorus, the lead in every solo), and **Use everywhere** plays it all through the track. The first card, **Auto** with the instrument the composer plays in that section, goes back to the composer's own. The drums' browser holds the kits, and the snare and cymbals for a kind of section. Nothing else about the tune changes, with one exception: a didgeridoo has one note, so the Didgeridoo (in the bass or the chords) or the Yidaki (in the chords or the melody) plays the part as a player would on that note. The bass becomes a drone on the key note, the chords a rhythm and the melody calls, sweeps and toots that follow its phrases. A link from before the two instruments that asks for the `yidaki` bass plays the Didgeridoo, whose drone it was. On a wide screen the browser is a panel beside the track, and on a phone a sheet from the bottom.
- **Favourite and blocked instruments.** The star on a card makes an instrument a favourite, which a shuffle draws more often. The block button keeps an instrument out of shuffles and new tunes: when the composer would play a blocked instrument in a new tune (another seed, style or length, or a shuffle), the generator plays another the style plays in its place and puts that choice in the link, so the link sounds the same for everyone. That includes a bass laid over another, such as the croak: the kind of section it was in then plays one bass the style has, with nothing laid over it, and a bass chosen for a kind of section plays that way too. A link that sets a blocked instrument still plays that instrument. Your favourites and blocks are kept with your profile.
- **Mute and solo.** Each track's head (each row on a phone) has mute, the speaker, and solo, the headphones: soloed tracks play alone. The star is only ever a favourite instrument, in the instrument browser.
- **Undo and redo.** Every change you make can be taken back: the genre, the seed, a shuffle, an instrument, mute and solo, a level, the tempo, the key, the length, the ear candy switches and **Reset**. Use the arrows beside **Render**, or Cmd or Ctrl+Z to undo and Shift+Cmd or Ctrl+Z or Ctrl+Y to redo. A slider dragged is one step, however far it moves. A playing track plays on, and the line under the track says what was undone or redone.
- **Playing.** Click the top row to play from a section, double-click a bar to play from that bar, and press space to play or stop. Zoom in, zoom out or fit the whole track; a wide screen shows more of the track.
- **On a phone.** Play, the time, the seed and shuffle sit along the bottom of the screen, the genre lock, the genre button and the link run across the top with undo, redo, **Render** and **Save** below them, and each track is a row showing the instruments it plays, then its mute and solo buttons. Tap a row to open its instruments. The visualiser shows while something plays, and the full track is below the rows.
- **Mix.** The Mix section holds the levels of each part, the ear candy (the risers, the console's sounds, the shouts, the birds, the sparkles, phasers and bleeps), the room, the texture, the brightness and the drive. Each is a pill with its name and value written on it: press anywhere along the pill to set the level or drag along the pill, and the arrow keys step the level. The Ear candy lane has its own mute and solo.
- **Render.** Play plays the track live, as fast as your device can work it out. **Render** opens three choices: **Render and play**, **Download M4A** and **Download WAV**. Each one works the whole track out first at full quality, with denser cymbals and more oversampling than live playing can afford, while a progress card shows how far it has got and about how long is left. A render can take longer than the track itself, and Cancel or Escape stops the render. Once the track is rendered the button reads **Rendered**, and each choice is immediate: the render plays straight away or the file downloads. A change to the track goes back to live playing until it is rendered again. A line under the choices says what every download records: Content Credentials naming the music generator, as procedural music with no AI.
- **Visualiser.** While a tune plays, or is stopped part way, a band above the track shows a spectrum analyser in the colour of the section playing. The settings button in its corner switches the band to a waveform, MilkDrop or off. **Expand** grows the band in the page and **Full screen** gives it the whole screen. Both open on MilkDrop, the same visualiser as the Neurospicy player, with its presets, a preset shuffle and its preset cycle, unless you prefer reduced motion, when they open on the analyser and MilkDrop waits for you to choose MilkDrop. In full screen the controls hide until you tap or move the pointer. Escape leaves full screen, then shrinks the band.
- **Keeping a track.** **Save** offers two places. **Save to assets** keeps the track as a tune: a few hundred bytes holding everything it was made with (its style and seed, instruments, tempo, key, mix and length), saved at once with nothing to render first. The tune plays in the Neurospicy player beside your own audio and in Assets with a player, and you can pick it as an audiogram's sound or a video's music bed (it loops to fill a longer video), always exactly as you made the track. **Save to projects** keeps every setting, so the track opens back in the music generator as you left the track. Files to share come from **Render**. Every setting is in the link, so the link button copies a track that anyone can open, play and change.

Escape closes the instrument browser or a panel, then leaves the generator. Focus goes back to the control that opened the browser.

## What a track records

A track the generator makes says that Lolly composed it, so nobody mistakes it for a recording or for AI output. The Make panel says what each write records before you click.

- **A saved tune.** **Save to library** records who made the tune, as the name in your profile under Settings → Preferences → Your details. With no name in the profile, no maker is recorded. The maker is a statement of who made the track, not a copyright claim. The tune also records that it was made in the music generator, and the Lolly and engine versions that made the track. Its licence starts as **Not recorded**. Open the tune in Assets to see each record in its details: **Source** reads *Made in the music generator*, with **Made by**, **Made with** and **Licence** below. The **Licence** menu sets a licence for your tune (CC0, CC BY, CC BY-SA and their NonCommercial and NoDerivatives forms), and **Not recorded** clears the licence. A tune saved from the Neurospicy player reads *Saved from the Neurospicy player* and records no maker.
- **A download.** Every WAV or M4A from **Download** carries [Content Credentials](/info/exporting.html#content-credentials-c2pa). The credential's created step says *Made with Lolly's music generator (procedural, no AI)*, then the style and the seed, and the credential carries no name. Its digital source type is algorithmic media: music computed by an algorithm, with no sampled or trained material, which Lolly Verify does not flag as AI. The same sentence is also the file's plain description, which a music player or a file inspector shows without reading the credential: the comment of a WAV, and the comment and description of an M4A. There is no switch to leave them out. A download whose credential did not read back is not handed over, and the line under the track says so in plain words. After a download, the line under the track repeats what a reading of the written file found.
- **A tune rendered from Projects.** Rendering a folder or a selection that holds a tune kept in Projects makes an M4A of each tune (a WAV where the browser has no AAC encoder), beside the folder's other files in the zip. Each one carries the same description and the same Content Credentials as a download, and a tune whose credential could not be written is reported as not made rather than delivered without one.
- **A tune in another file.** A video or an audio export that plays a saved tune (a video's music bed, an audiogram's sound, a clip on a timeline) records the tune as a source in its Content Credentials: its name, the licence when the tune records one, the SHA-256 of the tune's recipe, the engine version that rendered the tune, and algorithmic media. The maker's name joins that record only while **Use my details to create** is on under Settings → Preferences → Your details, the same switch your name follows everywhere else in an export; with the switch off the name stays on your device and the rest of the record is unchanged. Before you export, a line under **Download** lists the tunes and what the record will name, the maker included only when the name will be written; after the export, the same line reports what a reading of the delivered file found. Untick **C2PA Credentials** to leave the record out. A still image records no tune, because it carries no sound.

## Performance

The Lolly Sings genres play many more synthesised voices at once than the original Lolly Sings and the Lofi stations. On a genre's station, and on a saved tune from one, the player offers **Light visualiser** (the lightning bolt), which shows the simple level bars instead of the full visualiser so the music has the processing time it needs. The setting is on by default on a modest device: one with a touch screen, four processor cores or fewer, or 4 GB of memory or less. You can change it in the station's controls at any time.

Botanica renders its sounds of a place, its pads and its glitches as buffers while it plays: a ten-second loop of each bed, a few seconds of each instrument note its pads are cut from, and each pad and glitch once. Each is kept for the rest of the tune, so a progression that comes round again plays from what was already rendered.

The waves (Noosacore's, and any genre's with **Waves** on) are rendered the same way: each wave once, as its swell begins, and the shore's roar and the she-oaks' breeze as twenty-second loops for the tune.

## No samples, no recorded voices

Every sound is synthesised by Lolly's own code. A generated tune contains no samples, loops or recordings, and no recorded voice. The chopped breaks in Jungle, Breakbeat and some D&B, Hip hop and Trip hop tunes are no exception: each is Lolly's own drummer playing an original break on the modelled kit, recorded through a model of an old sampler as the tune starts and then chopped. No famous break is in them, and no break is copied from a record. The vocal sounds are built from the same oscillators and filters as every other instrument: the "hey" shout in Sugar, Reggae and K-pop; the shouts (hey, yeah, woo, oh, ayy, uh, ugh, whoa and yo), which Sherbert calls in series, hip hop and R&B use as ad-libs and punk's and ska's bands call together; and the vocal chops in Sherbert, R&B and lofi house, which sing syllables such as ah, oh, la, da and yeah with no words. The chops come from the formant voice that says the tag: a glottal pulse and breath through the resonances of a vocal tract. Each tune draws its own singer and the handful of syllables she sang, and each note is one slice of that, scooped, slid from the note before, flipped an octave, gated, stuttered or now and then reversed into a phrase, a little different every time. The harder a note is sung, the brighter and more open its vowel. The magpie and the kookaburra are synthesised too, each from a model of how the bird sings fitted to field recordings: the magpie's two-voiced carol of short notes and its pulsed warble, the kookaburra's raucous laugh from its low chuckles to its fall-away. In any other style, the Aussie birds switch under the ear candy in **Mix** adds them now and then.

Botanica's sounds of a place are models too, not field recordings. A bird sings syllables: a nearly pure whistle sweeping through each one, with the faint harmonics a bird's voice has, at the pace and in the range of its species. Wind is noise that swells and darkens with each gust, leaves are thousands of tiny contacts a second, running water and drips are bubbles ringing at their resonant pitch, rain is drops striking and landing in water, and crickets, cicadas and bees each sing, buzz or hum at their own rate. Each was measured against field recordings released into the public domain, and each is drawn from the tune's seed, so a tune's birds sing the same song every time.

The waves are a model as well. A wave is heard swelling (a low rumble growing for a second or two as the crest steepens and starts to spill), then breaking (a turbulent roar of water and the air it traps), then running up the sand as foam (a hiss of bubbles, each ringing at its own pitch, thinning as the foam spreads) and draining back as a quieter, brighter fizz. The break runs along the beach, so several stretches of the front break one after another across the stereo field. A wave further off is quieter, duller and narrower. The sea sends the waves in sets, a few bigger ones together, about seven to eleven seconds apart and never evenly, and the shore's roar runs under them. The model was fitted against field recordings released into the public domain (Gulf Shores in Alabama, an Australian beach at night, Gylly Beach in Cornwall, South Wollongong and Heceta Beach in Oregon), measured with the same code as the model. Against the Gulf Shores recording of medium surf, the model's waves came 6.5 seconds apart against 6.6, with a spread of 0.26 against 0.23; each rose 11.4 dB over the trough against 11.2, and fell 6 dB in 1.4 seconds against 1.7; the sound's centre of gravity sat at 644 Hz at the break against 621, and 775 Hz in the trough against 782; the octave bands from 63 Hz to 8 kHz were within about 1.5 dB; and the two channels correlated at 0.33 against 0.34. The river mouth's small waves, against a recording of waves lapping a shore (Freesound 659002), fell 6 dB in 0.86 seconds against 0.79 and rose 17 dB over the trough against 14.5.

The breeze in the she-oaks is a model too. A she-oak's leaves are fine jointed branchlets, a millimetre or less across, and air flowing past a thin cylinder sheds vortices from each side in turn, so each branchlet whistles at about a fifth of the wind's speed over its width. The tree sings a band of those whistles, all rising together when a gust gets up, over the broad sigh of the branches. Against a recording of a she-oak forest singing in the wind (Pink Lakes in Victoria, Freesound 266635), the model's band was centred at 632 Hz against 655, moved between 573 and 690 Hz against 580 and 747 as the gusts came and went, stood 5.2 dB over the noise around it against 5.7, came and went by 8.2 dB against 9.0, and sang higher as each gust got louder, as the trees do. The cicadas are botanica's.
