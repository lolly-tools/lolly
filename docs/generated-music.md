# Generated music

The Neurospicy player's generated stations compose each tune as the tune plays. Lolly's composer writes each tune and your browser synthesises the instruments. Nothing is recorded or sampled.

- **The Lolly Sings group.** The original Lolly Sings and its genres. Lolly 1.2.0 ships 26: Sugar, K-pop, Straya, Chiptune, City pop, Funk, Soul, Nu-disco, Hip hop, Trip hop, Reggae, Spanish guitar, Rock, Metal, Country, Blues, Jazz, Swing, Fantasy, Dreamscape, Synthwave, Acid, Electro, Dubstep, Brostep and D&B. Twenty-nine more arrived after 1.2.0: Sherbert, Persian, Guzheng, Koto, Kora, Afrobeat, Amapiano, Ska, Punk, R&B, Grunge, Stoner rock, Shoegaze, Lofi house, Salsa, Surf rock, Yacht rock, Botanica, Oompa, Mandopop, C-Pop, Breakbeat, Funky house, UK garage, Private school piano, Noosacore, Arnhem Land, Jungle and Trailer.
- **The Lofi group.** Three moods: Warm, Late night and Rainy.

The **music generator** opens any of these stations as a track you can change, down to the instrument each part plays in each section.

To start, read the [music generator guide](/info/music-generator.html), with pictures of every control, or play a sample of every genre in [Free background music](/info/background-music.html). Music you make in the generator is free for any use, including commercial use, provided you credit Lolly with the line **Music made with Lolly (https://lolly.tools), CC BY 4.0**.

For a brand's own sound, choose a starting sound in [Design System Setup’s Sound room](/info/brand-studio.html#sound), then shape each use with **Edit in the music generator**. A project made from the room keeps its use and the version of the sound. **New tune** composes a fresh tune with the same settings.

## Every tune is a number

A tune comes from its station and a **seed**, a whole number from 0 to 4,294,967,295. The composer turns the same station and seed into the same tune on every device: the same key, tempo, instruments, groove, running order and notes. The composer reads no clock, rolls no dice of its own and fetches nothing from the internet.

The music is deterministic. As a result:

- **A shared link plays the same tune.** The player's link button, **Share this tune or moment**, offers two links. **This tune** plays the tune from its start, for example `#/?lofi=sugar.2024`. **This moment** starts from the bar the tune had reached and shows the visualiser with its preset, colours, brand tint and preset cycle, for example `#/?lofi=sugar.2024&ns.at=11&ns.viz=pulse`. Either link plays your tune on the gallery, utilities or assets view the link names. The visualiser look applies to that visit only and replaces none of the viewer's own settings. Internet radio and catalog tracks share by their ids (`listen=…`), from the second you had reached. A track you uploaded stays on your device, so its card says so and offers no link.
- **A saved tune is a few bytes.** **Save to library** keeps the station and the seed as an asset of about 70 bytes, not a recording. Playing the tune composes the same music again. Lolly Demo and Lolly Start each ship six saved tunes.
- **A music bed renders the same audio.** A saved tune used as a video's music bed renders from the seed when the video is made and repeats to fill the video.
- **Next and Previous move between seeds.** Next starts a new tune with a fresh seed. Previous goes back to the seed before.

## What stays the same, and what can differ

![The station and the seed go to Lolly's composer, which reads no clock and rolls no dice, and the tune it writes is the same for everyone. Your browser synthesises the sound from that tune, with your own settings for drums, texture and volume applied on top.](/info/diagrams/concepts/tune-from-seed.svg)

**Stays the same for everyone:**

- The notes, their timing and their loudness
- The instruments, the tempo and the key
- The running order: intro, verses, builds, drops and the ending

**Can differ:**

- Your **Drums** and **Texture** settings, applied on top of the tune
- Your volume, your speakers and your headphones
- How an instrument sounds after a Lolly update that improves that instrument
- A percussion part (a shaker, a tambourine, congas) that an update adds to a genre; the other parts keep their notes
- New sounds a genre gains: Dubstep, Brostep and D&B end each phrase of a drop with the bass talking in place of the second half of that bar's bass line, with bleeps and phaser sweeps around the rest
- The bass in the electronic genres (November 2026): Dubstep, Brostep, D&B, Electro, Nu-disco and Lofi house play their bass as a stack, so every tune of theirs sounds different from before. Dubstep and Brostep drops trade their bass between characters. The croak calls over the bass in some Dubstep, Brostep, D&B and Sugar tunes instead of playing alone. So the bass notes changed in every Dubstep and Brostep tune, about four in ten D&B tunes and about six in ten Sugar tunes
- D&B's drums (November 2026): every drop edits the kick and the snare bar by bar (a kick moved or added, ghost notes and drags into the backbeat, half a bar re-cut, a turnaround to end each four bars and a fill into each eight), under a top loop: a drummer's break, chopped and played quietly. Every D&B tune's drops sound different from before; the rest of each tune is unchanged
- Chopped breaks (November 2026): every Breakbeat tune, about one D&B tune in three, about half the Hip hop tunes and about three Trip hop tunes in four play a chopped break: a drummer's two bars recorded once per tune through a model of an old sampler, cut into slices and replayed in a new order. In Breakbeat, Hip hop and Trip hop the break replaces the kit's drums; in D&B the break replaces the top loop, over the kit. Those tunes' drums sound different from before, and the rest of each tune keeps its notes. A Jungle tune's drums are its chopped break from the start
- Record scratches (November 2026): most Hip hop tunes, about one R&B tune in four, most Breakbeat and Jungle tunes, about one Trip hop tune in four, and now and then a Funk, Nu-disco or UK garage tune gain a DJ's scratches. The scratches are drawn on their own, so the rest of each tune keeps its notes
- The console's FM effects (November 2026): Sugar, Chiptune and Sherbert tunes gain spins, shots and a moment before each drop, and some K-pop and Synthwave tunes a moment before their last chorus or drop. These are drawn on their own, so the rest of each tune keeps its notes. A moment or a spin replaces the console sound at the end of that phrase (a level-up, a power-up, a coin or a jump)
- The soft lead, a simple synth line, is retired (November 2026). In most genres its share of the choices went to the modelled flute, which breathes and phrases like a player. City pop split its share between the flute and a trumpet, and Dreamscape between the flute and bells. Funk, Soul and Reggae gave theirs to a trumpet, Synthwave, Electro, Brostep and UK garage to bells, Amapiano to vibes, Shoegaze to a viola and Trip hop to a cello. Between one tune in twenty (Lofi house) and about four in ten (Funk and Shoegaze) now play a different lead. In the genres that change instruments between sections, most tunes changed somewhere, because their second leads and doublings were drawn again. A link or saved tune that chose the soft lead plays the flute
- The vocal chops' voices (November 2026): four voices measured from real singing sing the chops, a woman's, a man's, a baritone's and a child's, and each genre casts its own. Every chop sounds different from before. Sherbert and Sugar still sing a woman's voice, mostly sped up, so their chops keep their moves, except in an occasional breakdown sung by a children's choir. Tunes cast with a man, a baritone or a child (some R&B, hip hop, Mandopop, C-Pop and Arnhem Land tunes, and any genre's chops chosen by name) chop their notes a little differently, as that voice would. Women and men sing Salsa's coro and Arnhem Land's unison hook together. No genre's notes moved

A Lolly update can change how an instrument is synthesised, for example to fix a harsh sound or balance a mix. The tune keeps its notes, so a shared link still plays the same piece. New stations leave existing tunes unchanged, and the list above shows each genre that gains new parts. Lolly's tests pin known seeds to their tunes, so an update that rewrites an existing tune by accident fails before release.

## How a tune is made

Each station leans the composer towards its genre without fixing the outcome.

1. **A style.** The seed picks the instruments (keys, lead, bass and drum kit), how the drums are tuned, the groove, how often the chords change and how much tape wobble and room the tune gets.
2. **Harmony and melody.** The tune draws two chord progressions in its key and voices each chord close to the last. It writes a short melody and its answer from the key's pentatonic scale, with a chord tone on each strong beat.
3. **A running order.** Instruments enter, lead and drop out at different moments in each tune. An intro opens on the beat, the bass, a lead or the whole band. The Lolly Sings dance genres build tension with a roll and a riser, open the drop on a crash and change instruments between sections, with softer keys in the quiet parts and a second lead and bass in the second drop. The band genres play songs: verses, a pre-chorus that turns into the chorus with a fill round the toms, a solo and a last chorus. Blues plays twelve-bar choruses with the turnaround as its intro. Jazz and Swing trade four bars with the drummer.
4. **Sound.** Your browser plays the notes with oscillators, filters and noise. Sugar's slap bass uses frequency modulation, so the bass can be thumbed, popped, slid and bent. The horns brighten as they get louder, with breath that buzzes at the note's pitch. The plucked strings are modelled: a guitar, a banjo, a harp, an upright bass and the world styles' zithers and lutes each play a string of the right length that swings in two planes and loses its high overtones faster than its low ones. Each string is plucked at its own point, softer plucks are darker, and a nylon or flatwound string goes mellow sooner than a steel one. An electric guitar sounds through its pickup, the bridge pickup thinner and brighter than the neck pickup. Its distorted parts drive the amp with those strings, not with a synthesiser's waveform. The steel-string acoustic sounds through a body measured from recordings of real flat-tops: the air in the box near 110 Hz and the braced top's and back's resonances ring under every pluck, its high partials fade much sooner than an electric's and the open strings ring in sympathy. Playing noises are at the levels measured in recorded flatpicked phrases: the pick's click and scrape, frets ticking as fingers press and let go, fingers rubbing the strings between notes and on a shift up the neck, a squeak on a slide along a wound string and an occasional fret buzz on a hard stroke. As the lead, the guitarist decorates the tune as a flatpicker does: slides, hammer-ons and pull-offs, quick runs along the key's scale into a note, the chord strummed under a held note, double stops and muted strings raked into a note. About half the guitarists are percussive players, who knock the top on the backbeat of their strumming and on the beats the lead rests over. Strummed, the acoustic follows recorded strummers: the hand keeps moving, down on the beats and up between, about two strokes a beat; each stroke sweeps the strings in about 30 milliseconds; a held chord is strummed in the tune's own pattern (a boom-chick in country and Straya, sixteenths in the slow styles); the chord rings until the next stroke takes each string over; a percussive player chokes some backbeats into a muted chuck; and as the lead, about a quarter of the bars are strummed passages between the picked phrases. The band plays the composer's notes as people play, not exactly as written. The drummer plays in the style's pocket, with the snare laid back in hip hop and soul and the hats pushed in punk, and plays each hit a few milliseconds off the grid, the hats more than the kick. No two strokes are the same strength, and the hats lean on the beat. The machine styles stay tight. A guitarist fingers each chord on the fretboard, with open strings ringing as a folk player does or up the neck as a jazz player does. Each strum sweeps down from the low strings or up across the treble strings, faster when harder. A lead guitarist bends some long notes up into pitch and slides into some from the note before. The acoustic drums are modelled from the instruments before a tune starts. A cymbal is dozens of partials ringing at their own pitches, the high ones dying first, with the wash blooming just after the stick strikes. A ride has its bell. Hats are two plates, choked shut, and a closed hat cuts off an open one. Under every cymbal's partials, its far denser modes, too close to hear apart, ring as dozens of narrow bands of noise, so a crash is mostly wash and a ride keeps its ping. A drumhead rings at the membrane's own modes, starting sharp when struck hard and falling back into pitch. A tom is struck, not summed: the stick's contact with the head (shorter for a hard stroke, which reaches higher) sets its modes ringing, pulled closer together by the air inside, with the resonant head tuned slightly apart so the note beats and blooms, over the shell's short rings. A snare's wires buzz while the head moves, and buzz softly in sympathy when the kick or a tom is struck. Each drum is rendered at three strengths with three takes of each, so no two hits in a row are the same. The kit plays in its own room, where a snare rings out and drops off, and spreads across the speakers as overhead microphones hear it: hats to the left, the ride to the right. Electronic styles keep their synthesised metal, and each genre has its own hand percussion. In the game styles (chiptune, Sugar and Sherbert), the music shrinks as a breakdown starts and grows back as the breakdown ends, a 1-up crowns a drop, and coins ring, echo or come in pairs, brighter and longer the harder they play. A second console, in the style of the 16-bit machines' FM sound chip, joins in: four operators with feedback, pitches stepped once a video frame on the chip's frequency grid, square waves and noise from the second chip and the early consoles' gritty 9-bit converter and soft output filter. Before each drop comes one moment, a different one each time: a power-up climbing through the drop's chord, a warp diving three octaves across the speakers, a spin that charges up and fires a shot as the drop hits, a short start-of-game fanfare in two voices or the tape stopping under the music for the last beat. A spin revs up into some builds. A few laser shots answer the tune through each drop, off the beat while the lead holds a note, their echoes alternating sides and darker each time: a short shot, a pair, a long buzzing beam or a charged shot after a short spin. These are Lolly's own sounds in that style, not copies of any game's. Sherbert's builds mostly end on its chopped stutter, a bird or the tag, so its moments are rarer, and its shots go in the breakdown's spaces. K-pop and Synthwave borrow a moment before their last chorus or drop in about one tune in four. Most tunes have a producer's tag: a voice says "Lolly" once, at the top, as the moment before the first drop or chorus runs out, or at the end of the outro. The voice is synthesised, not recorded: a glottal pulse and breath through the formants of a vocal tract, with the dip of the second l that makes the word two syllables. The delivery suits the style: whispered or sung on the key in the calm styles, spoken or sung in jazz and soul, shouted by one person or a crowd in a band's. A club tune pitches the tag up or down, chops it into a stutter, says it like a robot or throws it into the echo. Each tune draws its own speaker, a lower or a higher voice, so the tags vary. The texture is a record's: tiny ticks of dust, each a short ring in one groove wall or the other so the tick is in stereo, an occasional lower pop, a scratch that ticks once a revolution on some records and the stylus's soft groove noise, swelling gently as the record turns, not a steady hiss.

::: details Where the newer instruments' sounds come from
The instruments of Persian, Guzheng, Koto, Kora and Amapiano follow published acoustics and openly licensed instrument models:

- plucked and struck strings shaped by where they are played;
- bars and drumheads as their measured modes (the balafon's from the Synthesis ToolKit's wood bar);
- breathy flutes and bowed strings through their bodies' resonances;
- the log drum after the MIT-licensed mda DX10's preset of that name.

A Persian dastgah's koron is played a quarter-tone flat, close to the neutral steps musicians play.

The saxophone and the clarinet are blown, not played from a waveform. Each follows Perry Cook's model of the instrument in the Synthesis ToolKit: the breath presses on a reed that closes as the pressure across it rises, and the reed drives a bore, a cone for the sax and a cylinder for the clarinet. The cone sounds every harmonic; the cylinder leads with the odd ones in its low register. Blown harder, the reed beats further and the note brightens and buzzes. You hear the wave leaving the sax's bell, so none of the cone's harmonics is missing. The sax's breath is heard in its tone, and its body is fitted to recorded altos and tenors. A tongued note speaks in about 25 milliseconds blown hard and 70 softly, a held note's pitch wanders by a couple of cents, and its vibrato comes in after a moment and grows, as recorded players' does. A Yacht rock saxophonist growls a few loud held notes and plays soft low ones in the breathy subtone. The brass and the flute start from their instruments' spectra (brass's even spread, the flute's near-pure tone) and get brighter the harder they are played. A note that starts as the one before ends is slurred: the instrument glides into the new pitch without a fresh attack. Every tune plays its own drum kit, drawn from its seed and its kind of music: an acoustic bass drum for a band, a feathered one for jazz, a dusty boom for lofi, an 808 or a punchier electronic kick for dance music, with each kit's snare wires, hats, ride, crash (or splash, or china) and toms. A tom struck hard starts up to 40 per cent sharp and falls back into pitch over a few tenths of a second, with the stick's click and the head's thud in its first 50 milliseconds, as recorded acoustic toms do. A drum struck harder is brighter as well as louder, and a ghost note is a soft dark tap. A jazz kit has a dark, washy ride with little bell, no china and small toms tuned high. Jazz and swing keep the hi-hat's foot on two and four under the ride, and their soloists lay back a little behind the beat.

Surf rock's spring reverb models a real tank: two springs carry high frequencies more slowly than low ones, so each echo arrives as a chirp, low end first and top a few tens of milliseconds later. That chirp is the drip after a picked note. Its echo times, decay at each frequency and tone were measured from a recorded two-spring tank, and its chirp from a recorded tank's impulse response. The surf amp's clean tone was fitted to a single-coil guitar recorded through a twin-speaker combo, and the tremolo picking's pace to recorded surf instrumentals. None of those recordings is in Lolly, only the numbers.

The Rhodes is modelled too: a struck tine rings in front of an electromagnetic pickup whose pull falls off with distance, so a soft note is nearly a pure tone and a hard one barks with strong low harmonics. Low notes are rich and the top of the keyboard nearly a sine, the harmonics die faster than the fundamental, and the tine's bell rings briefly as the hammer strikes. Its harmonics, decays, bell and attack were measured from recorded Rhodes notes. Surf rock's combo organ is built as a transistor organ: square waves divided down from twelve top-octave oscillators through its tabs, with the vibrato and the instant keying measured from two recorded combo organs.

The viola, the cello and the double bass are bowed like the fiddle: the hair grips the string until the string pulls free, slips back and is caught again, and the note sounds through the instrument's body. Each body was fitted to recorded notes of its own instrument. The air in the viola's box rings near 235 hertz, the cello's near 100 and the double bass's near 65, with the wood's first modes a little above, so a low note keeps only as much fundamental as the box lets through. A violist's stroke speaks in about a tenth of a second and a cellist's in about a fifth, as recorded strokes do. A violist uses a wide vibrato on nearly every held note, a cellist a narrower one and a bassist hardly any. As the lead, the cello plays the line an octave down and the double bass two octaves down, in their singing registers. The string section plays the keys' chords: each chord tone goes to the part whose range has the tone (basses, cellos, violas or violins), and each part is three players a few cents and a few milliseconds apart, each with their own vibrato, so a held chord is as wide as a recorded section's. The upright bass is the same double bass plucked: the string rings through the same body, its low harmonics outlast its high ones as a recorded bass's do, and the finger knocks the body as it lets go.

Sherbert was tuned by measuring three Australian future-bass records of the mid-2010s for tempo, groove, stereo width and tonal balance (134 to 145 beats a minute, half-time, chords as wide as the speakers over a mono sub bass). Sherbert copies none of their notes or sounds.

Sherbert's chords were rebuilt after listeners asked for more complexity. They were measured against seven openly licensed future-bass records (under CC BY 4.0, CC BY 3.0 and CC0, from the Internet Archive) and six CC0 chord loops from Freesound, for measurement only: none of their notes or sounds is used. Each chord note is a stack of seven saws (eleven when a tune is rendered), detuned on the uneven curve of the Roland JP-8000's supersaw and spread across the speakers, with a thinner stack an octave up, a short bright pluck on the attack, a breath of high noise on top and a dark body an octave under the lowest note. The chords share one chain, like a producer's chord bus: a high-pass that leaves the low end to the 808, a three-band compressor mixed under the dry chords (as producers use OTT, to bring up the detail between the hits), a soft clip, a chorus, a short room and a pump driven by the kick and, in a drop, every beat, so the room ducks with the chords. Against the loops, the chords' level moves 13 dB within a bar (the loops 15, the old chords 9), the low mids stay in the middle and the top spreads wide as in the loops, the attack stands about 7 dB over the chord a tenth of a second later (the loops about 5, the old chords 3), and each harmonic spreads about 20 cents wide (the loops about 21, the old chords 13). A drop's chords change from bar to bar: a third rhythm in every third bar, held chords gated in three-three-two or chopped in sixteenths, wubs at triplet and dotted rates, a stutter, a gated chord or a triplet run into each fourth bar, and a dive, a gap or a swell to end each eight-bar phrase. Some builds swell into the drop instead of pausing before the first chord. Sherbert's supersaw tunes play the same layered chord; the supersaw in Sugar, K-pop and the other styles is unchanged.

The bass stacks of Dubstep, Brostep, D&B, Electro, Nu-disco and Lofi house were tuned by measuring the drops of openly licensed records in those genres and in house and UK garage (from the Internet Archive and Jamendo, under Creative Commons licences or the artist's public domain mark, with CC0 bass loops from Freesound), for measurement only: none of their notes or sounds is used. In the recent records the low end is mono: below 120 Hz the difference between the two channels is 30 to 50 dB under the middle, and the bass stays mono up to about 200 Hz before spreading. Above that, the bass's middle is wide in Dubstep and D&B (channel correlation 0.6 to 0.8 from 150 Hz to 2 kHz) and narrower in house and garage (about 0.9 to 0.97). In Dubstep and D&B, 30 to 100 Hz has 7 to 10 dB more energy than 150 Hz to 2 kHz. The music dips 7 to 16 dB under each kick and recovers in about a quarter of a second, and the wobbles move at two, three or four swings a beat, locked to the tempo. Bass loops made to sit over a sub carry little under 150 Hz and are wide; full basses carry most of their energy under 150 Hz. Lolly's basses were one channel all the way up. Now the low end measures as the records do (the side 35 to 45 dB down below 120 Hz), the middle spreads (correlation 0.69 to 0.86 in Dubstep, Brostep and D&B, about 0.95 in the house styles) and the low end sits about 8 dB over the middle in Dubstep and D&B. Each style's bass stays within half a decibel of its old level against the mix. Sugar keeps its slap bass alone: the croak, which played alone in some Sugar verses before, carried only 40 per cent of its energy under 150 Hz where the slap and the full bass loops carry 70 to 90, so the croak now calls over the slap.

Salsa's grand piano and Latin percussion are models too, worked out note by note and measured against recordings of real instruments. Nothing recorded is shipped. The piano was fitted to two recorded grand pianos at several strengths: the hammer reaching further up the string the harder it strikes, the stiff string's sharpened partials, the three strings of a note beating and dying away at two rates, the soundboard's knock and the damper. The congas, bongos, timbales, bells, clave and güiro were fitted to recorded single strokes: each drumhead's modes and ring time, the hand's slap, the stick on the shell, the güiro's uneven ridges and the stick rubbing between them, rung through the gourd, and the maracas' seeds striking their shells. The mix was balanced against four recorded salsa bands, taking only the style's traits: no song, arrangement or artist is copied.

Oompa's band is blown, not sampled. The tuba, the flugelhorn, the baritone and the tenor horn use the trumpet's lip model with bores of their own: the lips are a mass on a spring that the breath pushes open, driving a bore whose bell lets its harmonics go from its own cutoff, a few hundred hertz on a tuba and about a kilohertz on a flugelhorn. Each was fitted to recordings of the real instrument at several strengths. The tuba was fitted to the Philharmonia's tuba notes, F1 to B♭2, pianissimo to fortissimo: its first ten harmonics come within 6.4 dB of the recordings on average (the trombone it would otherwise borrow was 18.3 dB away). The flugelhorn was fitted to two recorded players (9.6 dB, the trumpet 18.0) and the baritone to a recorded euphonium (6.7 dB, the trombone 8.8). No tenor horn was found under a licence that allows measuring, so the tenor horn is the baritone's bore with a smaller bell. A big bore's loop takes a third of a second to build alone, far slower than a player, so the band's notes speak as recorded players' do: a soft tuba note in about a fifth of a second and a loud one in a few hundredths. The clarinet's upper register was refitted against recorded clarinets: above the break its second harmonic now rises as a real clarinet's does, within 4.8 dB of the University of Iowa's E♭ clarinet where the old model was 7.0 dB away. Every player has their own tuning and place against the beat, the whole band drifts a little ahead and behind as one, every tongued note starts with the air the tongue releases, a player who has played a while breathes in the next rest, and the band plays in a hall whose sound dies away in 0.8 to 0.9 seconds below a kilohertz. Measured the same way as five recorded Bavarian and Austrian bands (field recordings and a rehearsal, under Creative Commons licences or the public domain mark, measurement only), the oom sounds 24 ms after the beat (the recorded bands 19 to 26 ms), the after-beat half a beat after it (0.53 to 0.65), the players' partials spread 7 cents (3 to 14) and the mix's octave bands sit within about 1.5 dB of the recorded bands' middle from 125 Hz to 8 kHz.

The erhu and the dizi of Guzheng, Mandopop and C-Pop are modelled and fitted to openly licensed recordings, for measurement only: nothing recorded is shipped. The erhu is the fiddle's bowed string heard through a python skin over a small box. The skin gives a weak low fundamental, a nasal peak between 1.6 and 1.9 kHz and a bright edge above 3 kHz. With no fingerboard, a stopped string ends on the finger's soft pad, which rounds each wave, while the two open strings ring from the nut. The bow's hair passes between the two strings and damps the string it is not playing. Fitted to the ErhuPT dataset, four Freesound recordings and the FolkMusic dataset's erhu clips, its held harmonics come within 3.2 dB of the recordings on average, register by register. Over the same phrases its long-term spectrum is 4.6 to 9 dB from each recording, where the voice it replaced was 10 to 18 dB away. A stroke speaks in about 20 milliseconds loud and 50 soft, as recorded strokes do. The player uses a slow, wide vibrato on most long notes, slides along the string between notes and into the notes the tune bends, and lets some held notes fall off into a rest. The dizi is the jet flute with its dimo, the thin membrane over a hole near the mouth hole. Driven by the bore, the membrane buzzes and adds odd harmonics, which a recorded dizi carries only 11 to 16 dB under the whole and a Western flute much lower. Fitted to the FolkMusic dataset's dizi and to Freesound notes and phrases, its harmonics come within 4.3 dB of the recordings (the flute alone is 14.1 dB away). Its player flicks grace notes from the hole above, slides off a hole into a note, turns round a note, flutter-tongues a long, loud note at about 18 a second and falls off a note before a breath.

Breakbeat, Funky house, UK garage and Private school piano were tuned by measuring 27 openly licensed records from the Internet Archive in those styles, in amapiano and in house (under Creative Commons licences, some of them non-commercial or no-derivatives, for measurement only: none of their notes or sounds is used). Each was measured over 16 bars of its fullest section. The breakbeat records loop a one- or two-bar break and edit the loop at the end of a phrase: in most of them no bar's drums differ from the bar before by much (a cosine distance of 0.15 between the bars' onsets), and at most a quarter of the bars do. Their sixteenths are nearly straight (the off-beat sixteenth at 0.49 to 0.61 of the eighth). Funky house swings a little (0.49 to 0.62) and repeats its bars, UK garage swings hardest (0.50 to 0.65, the speed garage records 0.65), and a true two-step keeps its kick off beat three. The amapiano records sit at 111 to 117 beats a minute and swing 0.50 to 0.67. Under 150 Hz the mix sits 2.3 to 6.6 dB under the whole in breakbeat, 0.7 to 6.1 in funky house, 1.8 to 3.9 in UK garage and 2.7 to 7.9 in amapiano, and the music dips 6 to 14 dB under each kick. Lolly's breaks first changed nearly every bar (0.8 to 1.0 of the bars, by the same measure). They now loop their first bar and turn at the end of each eight, with a drag on the snare to end each four: 0.14 to 0.21 of the bars change. The garage drums are a programmed two-bar loop that a fill turns every eight, as in the records. D&B's drums alone once changed in none to 0.07 of a drop's bars, where the two measured D&B records change 0.27 and 0.80, their kick and snare moving around the two-step. Its drops now edit the kick and the snare bar by bar around the same two-step, with a turnaround every four bars and a fill every eight: 0.43 to 0.79 of the bars change, with 6 to 9 different bars in 16 (the records 7 and 9).

The chopped breaks and Jungle were fitted to openly licensed recordings, for measurement only: none of their notes or sounds is used, and none of the famous breaks was measured or copied. Twelve takes of drummers playing funk in a room (Freesound, CC0 and CC BY) set the drummer: their sixteenths are nearly straight (0.50 of the eighth, the hats a little more swung), the backbeat sits on the beat (a median of a third of a millisecond late), and the ghost notes come about two a bar, 17 dB under the backbeat. The modelled kit, recorded the same way, at first had almost no room (a snare's tail fell 20 dB in 22 ms where the takes' fall in about 80) and a hole between 500 Hz and 2 kHz. Its room, desk and snare were set until its octave bands sat within about 2 dB of the takes' between 63 Hz and 8 kHz and its snare's tail fell 20 dB in 75 to 140 ms. Nine sampler-era breaks, four of them made on an SP-1200, set the sampler: a steep filter near 11 kHz, almost nothing above 13 kHz (57 dB down), and the record's noise about 35 dB under the hits. Lolly's twelve-bit samplers measure 57 dB down above 13 kHz with their noise 33 to 37 dB under. Eight jungle records' drops sit at 170 to 193 beats a minute with the sub 3.5 LU under the breaks, K-weighted (from 11 under to level); Lolly's jungle drops sit 1.8 to 3.7 under. The records loop their breaks between edits, a bar in eight to a bar in two unlike the bars before; Lolly's first drop edits about a bar in three and its second about a bar in two. The other styles' breaks were set against their records the same way: the sub 1 to 2 LU under the breaks in breakbeat, level with them in hip-hop, and 2 to 4 under in trip-hop.

The turntable was fitted to 48 openly licensed recordings of real scratching from Freesound (CC0 and CC BY, for measurement only: none of their sound is used, and any that scratched a famous record or a famous scratch sample was turned away). Four of them scratch a steady tone, which gives the record's speed exactly: the hand's strokes peak at 1.6 to 2.3 times the record's own speed, and between strokes the record plays at its own speed. Lolly's baby scratches and flares peak at 1.9 and its transformers' slower strokes at 1.6. Across all 40 scratch recordings a stroke lasts about 120 ms, five or six to a second, its speed peaking about 0.4 of the way through; Lolly's sixteenth strokes at 90 beats a minute come six to a second and peak at 0.42. The crossfader cuts in over about 3 ms and out over about 3.75 ms (Lolly's, measured the same way, 2.75 and 3.5), and when shut still passes the sound about 48 dB down, as Lolly's does. A real fader's cut-in clicks no more than a smooth fade, so the modelled fader's click is small. A transformer's sound comes in pieces of about 120 ms with gaps of 50 to 150 ms; Lolly's pieces are 121 ms, with slightly shorter gaps of 46 ms. Thrown backspins reach full speed in 120 to 200 ms and slow with a time constant of 0.37 to 0.49 s, and the one followed to the end fell to a tenth of full speed in 0.62 s; Lolly's reach theirs in 135 ms, slow with a time constant of 0.31 s and fall to a tenth in 0.69 s. The recordings' sound is centred near 1.2 kHz and falls 20 dB by about 4 kHz. Lolly's scratched voice is centred near 940 Hz and its horn near 1.8 kHz, through a cartridge that rounds off the top near 6.5 kHz. In a tune a held scratch sits 1.5 to 5 LU under the hook at its loudest (K-weighted, over 400 ms) and a short stab or chirp lower still; a rewind, which holds the record at speed longer than any other scratch, plays lower to sit under the hook too.

Below them are a visualiser and the track, laid out like a sequence editor: sections and chords along the top, a lane for each part and every note drawn. Sections take their genre's names: a band's drop is its chorus, its break the solo.

The lanes are **Melody** (the tune), **Chords** (guitars, strings, harps and saw chords as often as keys), **Bass**, **Drums** and **Ear candy** (sounds laid over the tune: birds, waves, shouts, the Lolly tag, a games console, sparkles, sweeps and a DJ's scratches). Links use the older ids (`lead`, `keys`, `bass`, `drums` and `fx`), so links made before the names changed play as they did.

**Make a track** in four steps:

::: steps
- <!--i:eti-sparkles--> **Pick a start** Choose a genre and a seed, or shuffle for both. Set the key and tempo if you like.
- <!--i:eti-layers--> **Change instruments by section** Click a section in a lane, hear the instruments in that section and use one there or all through the track.
- <!--i:eti-sliders--> **Mix the parts** Set each part's level, the effects, the room and the drive.
- <!--i:eti-download--> **Keep the track** Save the tune to your library, or download an M4A or WAV file.

The controls:

- **Drums and ear candy.** The drums track's browser replaces the genre's kit with another (acoustic, jazz, dusty, hip-hop, electronic or 808). The lane head shows the genre's own kit as **Auto** and the kit's name (**Auto · Jazz kit**, **Auto · Straya kit**). Clicking a kit plays two bars of the tune's drums on that kit. A kit changes how the drums sound, never which notes they play. The Ear candy track's menu chooses how the tune says "Lolly": **Auto** (the composer's placement and delivery), not at all or one delivery for every tag, placed at the top when the composer placed none. In **Mix**, the ear candy switches turn off the voices (the shouts and the tag), the console's sounds (with its FM spins, shots and moments), the sparkles, the sweeps (with a DJ's scratches), the birds (**Aussie birds**: the magpie, the kookaburra, the whipbird and the lorikeets) or the waves. In a genre without birds or waves of its own, those two switches add them: **Aussie birds** adds the magpie and the kookaburra (and sometimes a whipbird pair or a flock of lorikeets flying over), and **Waves** adds one to three soft sea moments a tune: a wave breaking on the first beat of a drop or into the break, or the shore's roar swelling for a few bars. Noosacore has both on, and there **Waves** also switches the she-oaks' breeze on and off. The link carries each choice.
- **Ear candy moments.** Each sound on the Ear candy lane shows as a small icon where the sound enters: a bird, waves, a games console, a riser or a sweep, a shout or the Lolly tag, sparkles, the sounds of a place or a record for a DJ's scratch. A sound that repeats bar after bar has one icon at its start. Point at an icon, or move to one with the keyboard, to see its name. Press one while the track is stopped to hear that moment alone; press one while the track plays to continue from that moment's bar. The icons are one Tab stop, and the arrow keys move from moment to moment through the track, scrolling as they go.
- **The melody's place.** Each genre sets how far the melody stands out from the band: further back in the calm styles, forward in jazz and swing. Each instrument is first levelled against the others, so a bright synth lead and a quiet flute sit in the same place. An instrument you choose for the melody takes that place too.
- **Length.** A track holds whole tunes and ends as a song does. Where your length falls partway through a tune, that tune is cut at the end of a phrase and finished with its own outro and fade. A build always runs into its drop before any cut. So the length can come out longer or shorter than you chose: a tune that would lose only its last few bars plays to the end, and a later tune that would get fewer than 16 bars before its outro is left out.
- **Genre.** The genre button shows the genre playing: its icon, its name and a line of what you hear (Straya's reads "Didgeridoo, banjo and bush birds"), in a stronger form of its picker card's colours from your design system. On a light theme a deep colour carries light text; the text keeps at least 4.5:1 contrast on every colour, in light and dark. When the genre changes, by a shuffle, the picker, an undo or a link, the button lights up briefly; with reduced motion on, in your system's settings or in Lolly's, the button changes without moving. Clicking the button opens every genre as a card with an icon, each in its own wash of your design system's colours, A to Z under Chill (with the Lofi stations), Rock & Roots, Soul & Groove, Club, Bass & Synth and World. A **Favourites** row at the top holds the genres you starred, and a **Recent** row under it the last six genres you played, newest first. Both follow your profile like your instrument preferences, and each appears once it has a genre. **Manage**, in the picker's heading, lists every genre with a star and an eye. Star a genre to keep it in Favourites and hear it more often. Hide a genre to leave it out of the picker and every shuffle (a link, or a closed padlock, still plays a hidden genre). Each change saves at once, and its note has **Undo**. The search field narrows the cards to titles with a word that starts with what you typed, so "house" finds Lofi house and Funky house; clear the field to see the groups again. Click a card, or move with the arrow keys and press Enter, to play that genre. Escape clears the search, then closes the picker.
- **Shuffle.** The round button with crossing arrows, beside play (at the bottom of the screen on a phone), draws a new genre, a new seed and a sound to suit them. Some parts keep the composer's instrument and some get another the style plays, sometimes a kind of section gets its own instrument, and the mix, texture, brightness and drive move a little either side of the style's own. The tempo returns to the tune's own and the length stays yours. The padlock between shuffle and the genre button (next to the genre button on a phone) sets whether shuffle may change the genre: open, shuffle draws a new genre; closed, shuffle keeps the genre and draws only a new seed and sound. The line under the track says which, and your profile keeps the setting. A closed padlock also goes into the link (`lock=genre`), so whoever opens the link shuffles within the same genre until they open the padlock. Hold Shift to keep the genre, the seed and the key, and shuffle only the sound. A playing track plays on in the new one. With the padlock open, a starred genre is three times as likely as any other; with none starred, shuffle draws every genre alike.
- **Opening.** The generator opens on a track drawn at random, as **Shuffle** draws one. A link to a track opens that track.
- **Reset** keeps the style and the seed and puts every other setting back to the composer's own: 64 bars, every part automatic, the tune's own key and tempo, every level at its designed level, and nothing muted or soloed.
- **The seed.** Type any seed, step with the arrows, or drag across the number to slide through seeds and watch the tune change.
- **The key.** The key menu moves the tune up to six semitones up or down and lists each key by name. Every pitched part moves together; the drums stay as they are.
- **The tempo.** Each tune plays at its own tempo until you set one: type beats a minute in the tempo field, or drag across the field. Only the speed changes; the notes keep their places in the bar. The arrow next to the tempo field returns to the tune's own tempo. The player always plays a station at the tune's own tempo.
- **Instruments.** Each instrument draws its notes in its own shade of the part's colour. Where a part changes instrument, a line of the new shade runs down the lane and a chip shows the instrument's family icon and name. The chip is filled when you chose the instrument and tinted when the composer did. Each lane's head shows the instrument playing at the playhead: its name when you chose it, and **Auto** with its name when the composer did (**Auto · Yidaki**).
- **The instrument browser.** A track's head (its row on a phone) or a section in its lane opens the browser for the melody, the chords or the bass. On a wide screen the browser is a panel beside the track that grows out of what you clicked and shrinks back there when it closes; on a phone the browser is a sheet that rises from the bottom. Its heading says where the choice applies ("Chords · Chorus", or "Chords · every section" from the lane head), with what plays there now under it ("Auto · Banjo", or the instrument you chose). The lane, or every section of that kind in the lane, lights up while the browser is open. Click another section to move the browser there. The chips under the heading choose every section or one kind of section. Every instrument is a card, grouped by family (keys, guitars and strings, bells and mallets, winds and brass, voices, world, synths and 8-bit). The voices hold the vocal chops: **Vocal chops** (the genre's own choice of voice), each voice alone (female, male, baritone and child), a woman with a baritone an octave under her, a children's choir and a mixed group. Click a card to hear two bars of this tune's part on that instrument, in the chosen kind of section, with the other parts turned down. **Use in every Chorus** (named for the chosen section) plays that instrument in every section of that kind, and **Use everywhere** plays it all through the track; the button for where you came from comes first. The first card, **Auto** with the composer's instrument for that section, returns to the composer's choice. The drums' browser holds the kits, and the snare and cymbals for a kind of section. Nothing else about the tune changes, with one exception: a didgeridoo has one note, so the Didgeridoo (in the bass or the chords) or the Yidaki (in the chords or the melody) plays the part as a player would on that note. The bass becomes a drone on the key note, the chords a rhythm and the melody calls, sweeps and toots that follow its phrases. A link from before the two instruments that asks for the `yidaki` bass plays the Didgeridoo, whose drone it was. The **Turntable**, among the melody's synths, plays the melody as a DJ would scratch it, on a record of the tune's own: a vowel the chop voice sings, a horn stab or a bass note. A note's length chooses the scratch: short notes are stabs and chirps, longer ones flares, baby scratches and transformers. The record is pitched to each melody note, so the scratches follow the key.
- **Favourite and blocked instruments.** The star on a card makes an instrument a favourite, which a shuffle draws more often. The block button keeps an instrument out of shuffles and new tunes. When the composer would play a blocked instrument in a new tune (another seed, style or length, or a shuffle), the generator plays another instrument of the style and puts that choice in the link, so the link sounds the same for everyone. This includes a bass laid over another, such as the croak: that kind of section then plays one of the style's basses with nothing laid over it, and a bass chosen for a kind of section also plays alone. A link that sets a blocked instrument still plays that instrument. Your profile keeps your favourites and blocks. **Your instruments**, in the browser's heading, lists every instrument you starred or blocked for the melody, the chords and the bass, each with one button to remove the star or the block, and **Undo** in the note that follows.
- **Mute and solo.** Each track's head (each row on a phone) has mute (the speaker) and solo (the headphones). Soloed tracks play alone. The star only marks a favourite instrument, in the instrument browser.
- **Undo and redo.** You can undo every change: the genre, the seed, a shuffle, an instrument, mute and solo, a level, the tempo, the key, the length, the ear candy switches and **Reset**. Use the arrows beside **Render**, or Cmd or Ctrl+Z to undo and Shift+Cmd or Ctrl+Z or Ctrl+Y to redo. A slider drag is one step, however far it moves. A playing track plays on, and the line under the track says what was undone or redone.
- **Playing.** Click the top row to play from a section, double-click a bar to play from that bar, and press space to play or stop. Zoom in, zoom out or fit the whole track; a wide screen shows more of the track.
- **On a phone.** The dock at the bottom has the controls you press often: play, the seed, shuffle, **Render**, **Save** and the link. Along the top of the dock runs the seek bar, with the elapsed time at one end, the track's length at the other and the tune's sections drawn in their colours. Tap or drag along the seek bar to go to a bar: a playing track carries on from the bar you release on; a stopped track starts there. The seek bar moves by whole bars, as the Neurospicy player's does, so the music stays in time. With the keyboard, the arrow keys step a bar, Page Up and Page Down a section, and Home and End go to the start and the end; a screen reader hears the bar, the section and the time. The genre lock, the genre button, undo and redo run across the top. Each track is a row showing its instruments, then its mute and solo buttons. Tap a row to open its instruments. The visualiser shows while something plays, and the full track is below the rows.
- **Mix.** The Mix section has each part's level, the ear candy (the risers, the console's sounds, the shouts, the birds, the sparkles, phasers and bleeps), the room, the texture, the brightness and the drive. Each is a pill showing its name and value: press or drag along the pill to set the level, or step the level with the arrow keys. The Ear candy lane has its own mute and solo.
- **Render.** Play plays the track live, computed as fast as your device can. **Render** opens three choices: **Render and play**, **Download M4A** and **Download WAV**. Each first computes the whole track at full quality, with denser cymbals and more oversampling than live playing allows, while a progress card shows progress and about how long is left. A render can take longer than the track, and Cancel or Escape stops the render. Once the track is rendered the button reads **Rendered**, and each choice is immediate. A change to the track returns to live playing until the next render. A line under the choices says what every download records: Content Credentials naming the music generator, as procedural music with no AI.
- **Motion.** Sections fold open and shut, the visualiser's band grows in and folds away, menus and sheets open from their buttons, the instrument and genre cards cascade in as the tools and utilities do, undo and redo give a small turn, and a change to the notes (an instrument, a mute, a new tune) fades the track to its new picture. With reduced motion on, in your system's settings or in Lolly's, or with the performance setting on, everything changes at once.
- **Visualiser.** While a tune plays, or is stopped part way, a band above the track shows a spectrum analyser in the colour of the current section. The settings button in its corner switches the band to a waveform, MilkDrop or off. **Expand** grows the band in the page and **Full screen** fills the screen. Both open on MilkDrop, the Neurospicy player's visualiser, with its presets, preset shuffle and preset cycle. With reduced motion on, they open on the analyser until you choose MilkDrop. In full screen the controls hide until you tap or move the pointer. Escape leaves full screen, then shrinks the band.
- **Keeping a track.** **Save** offers two places. **Save to assets** keeps the track as a tune of a few hundred bytes that has its style and seed, instruments, tempo, key, mix and length. Saving is immediate, with nothing to render. The tune plays in the Neurospicy player beside your own audio and in Assets, and you can pick the tune as an audiogram's sound or a video's music bed (looping to fill a longer video), always as you made the track. **Save to projects** keeps every setting, so the track reopens in the music generator as you left the track. Files to share come from **Render**. Every setting is in the link, so the link button copies a track that anyone can open, play and change.

Escape closes the instrument browser or a panel, then leaves the generator. Focus goes back to the control that opened the browser.

## Performance

The Lolly Sings genres play many more synthesised voices at once than the original Lolly Sings and the Lofi stations. On a genre's station, or a saved tune from one, the player offers **Light visualiser** (the lightning bolt): simple level bars in place of the full visualiser, leaving the music the processing time it needs. The setting is on by default on a device with a touch screen, four processor cores or fewer, or 4 GB of memory or less. Change the setting in the station's controls at any time.

The vocal chops are sung into buffers, each slice once, the first time a tune needs that slice. Each voice costs within about a tenth of the old single voice, and a voice in a choir or a group is one take, not two. So a children's choir of five costs about what the old three-voice coro did, and salsa's coro now costs less.

While Botanica plays, it renders its place sounds, pads and glitches as buffers: a ten-second loop of each place sound (played for a few short passages), a few seconds of each instrument note its pads are cut from, and each pad and glitch once. Each buffer is kept for the rest of the tune, so a repeated progression plays from what was already rendered.

The waves (Noosacore's, and any genre's with **Waves** on) are rendered the same way: each wave once, as its swell begins, and the shore's roar and the she-oaks' breeze as twenty-second loops, each played as a short swell.

## No samples, no recorded voices

Lolly's own code synthesises every sound. A generated tune contains no samples, loops or recordings, and no recorded voice. The turntable scratches Lolly's own sounds (a vowel the chop voice sings, a horn stab blown by the trumpet model, the tune's own chopped break, a bass note), never a record and never a famous scratch sample. Each chopped break in Jungle, Breakbeat and some D&B, Hip hop and Trip hop tunes is Lolly's own drummer playing an original break on the modelled kit, recorded through a model of an old sampler as the tune starts and then chopped. No famous break is in them, and no break is copied from a record. The vocal sounds use the same oscillators and filters as every other instrument: the "hey" shout in Sugar, Reggae and K-pop; the shouts (hey, yeah, woo, oh, ayy, uh, ugh, whoa and yo), which Sherbert calls in series, hip hop and R&B use as ad-libs and punk's and ska's bands call together; and the vocal chops in Sherbert, R&B and lofi house, which sing syllables such as ah, oh, la, da and yeah with no words. The chops come from the formant voice that says the tag: a glottal pulse and breath through the resonances of a vocal tract. Each tune draws its own singer and a handful of syllables. Each note is one slice, scooped, slid from the note before, flipped an octave, gated, stuttered or sometimes reversed into a phrase, a little different every time. The harder a note is sung, the brighter and more open its vowel. The magpie and the kookaburra are synthesised too, each from a model of the bird's song fitted to field recordings: the magpie's two-voiced carol of short notes and its pulsed warble, the kookaburra's raucous laugh from its low chuckles to its fall-away. In other styles, the Aussie birds switch in **Mix** adds them now and then.

The chops sing in four voices, each measured from real singing: a woman's (a soprano or a mezzo), a man's (a tenor), a baritone's and a child's. A voice is its vocal tract (the formants of each vowel for that kind of speaker, from Hillenbrand and colleagues' 1995 averages for men, women and children of ten to twelve), its range and where it turns over (a man breaks into falsetto at the top, open, breathy and quieter, where his ring disappears), how its glottis sounds soft and loud, its wobble from one period to the next, its vibrato and, in the trained adult voices, the ring of the third and fourth formants drawn together near 2.6 kHz (a baritone) or 2.9 kHz (a tenor). The adult voices were fitted to VocalSet (Wilkins and colleagues, 2018, CC BY 4.0), twenty trained singers on long tones, scales and arpeggios, and the child's to openly licensed recordings of children singing, solo and in choirs (Freesound CC0, Wikimedia Commons CC BY and public domain). The recordings were measured, never used: each model voice sang the same vowels at the same pitches, measured the same way, until the numbers matched. The woman's vibrato is about 5.6 cycles a second, 40 cents either way; the man's 5.4; the baritone's 5.1; the child's a slow waver near 4 on fewer than half the notes. Trained singers' vibrato and ring are about twice a pop singer's, so the chops keep about half of each. A man's and a baritone's chops sing an octave under the melody (a baritone two in a bar that sits high), flip less and slide more slowly; a child's are lighter, with few falls or slides. A children's choir is five children, each with their own take, timing and place across the speakers, and a group is two women with a man and a baritone under them. No real singer is imitated, and the syllables stay wordless whoever sings them.

Botanica's sounds of a place are models too, not field recordings. A bird sings syllables: a nearly pure whistle sweeping through each one, with a bird voice's faint harmonics, at its species' pace and range. Wind is noise that swells and darkens with each gust, leaves are thousands of tiny contacts a second, running water and drips are bubbles ringing at their resonant pitch, rain is drops striking and landing in water, and crickets, cicadas and bees each sing, buzz or hum at their own rate. Each was measured against field recordings released into the public domain and is drawn from the tune's seed, so a tune's birds sing the same song every time.

The waves are a model too. A wave swells (a low rumble growing for a second or two as the crest steepens and starts to spill), breaks (a turbulent roar of water and the air it traps), runs up the sand as foam (a hiss of bubbles, each ringing at its own pitch, thinning as the foam spreads) and drains back as a quieter, brighter fizz. The break runs along the beach, so stretches of the front break one after another across the stereo field. A wave further off is quieter, duller and narrower. A real sea sends waves in sets of a few bigger ones, about seven to eleven seconds apart and never evenly, under the shore's roar; the music plays only a few of those waves, and the roar only as a short swell. The model was fitted against field recordings released into the public domain (Gulf Shores in Alabama, an Australian beach at night, Gylly Beach in Cornwall, South Wollongong and Heceta Beach in Oregon), measured with the same code as the model. Against the Gulf Shores recording of medium surf, the model's waves came 6.5 seconds apart against 6.6, with a spread of 0.26 against 0.23; each rose 11.4 dB over the trough against 11.2, and fell 6 dB in 1.4 seconds against 1.7; the sound's centre of gravity sat at 644 Hz at the break against 621, and 775 Hz in the trough against 782; the octave bands from 63 Hz to 8 kHz were within about 1.5 dB; and the two channels correlated at 0.33 against 0.34. The river mouth's small waves, against a recording of waves lapping a shore (Freesound 659002), fell 6 dB in 0.86 seconds against 0.79 and rose 17 dB over the trough against 14.5.

The breeze in the she-oaks is a model too. A she-oak's leaves are fine jointed branchlets, a millimetre or less across, and air flowing past a thin cylinder sheds vortices from each side in turn, so each branchlet whistles at about a fifth of the wind's speed over its width. The tree sings a band of those whistles, all rising together when a gust gets up, over the broad sigh of the branches. Against a recording of a she-oak forest singing in the wind (Pink Lakes in Victoria, Freesound 266635), the model's band was centred at 632 Hz against 655, moved between 573 and 690 Hz against 580 and 747 as the gusts came and went, stood 5.2 dB over the noise around it against 5.7, came and went by 8.2 dB against 9.0, and sang higher as each gust got louder, as the trees do. The cicadas are Botanica's.

The whipbird and the rainbow lorikeets are models too. A whipbird pair calls as one bird: the male holds a pure whistle, then a sweep from low under the whistle turns near vertical and cracks into a burst of broadband noise with a sharp top edge, and the female answers with one or two quick notes, each a broad band of noise falling from about 5 kHz. Against five recordings of eastern whipbirds (Terrey Hills, the Central Coast, a forest recording, Mount Tamborine and a public-domain recording on Wikimedia Commons, 16 calls), the model's whistle lasted 1.3 seconds against 1.1, sat at 1.94 kHz against 2.07, with its second harmonic 40 dB down against 39; the crack ran from 340 Hz to 8.8 kHz against 390 Hz to 8.3 kHz, lasted 0.20 seconds against 0.26 and rose in 26 ms against 22; the female answered 0.21 seconds after the crack against 0.25, her notes falling in their centre from about 3.0 to 2.6 kHz against 3.7 to 2.9; and pairs called about 9.7 seconds apart against 9.5. A lorikeet's call is a buzzy voice near 2 kHz rich in harmonics, its pitch jumping and wobbling within the call. Against a flock feeding on lilly pilly (Freesound 385952) and birds at a feeder (75267), the model's calls came 1.7 and 1.3 a second against 1.4 and 1.2, lasted 188 and 371 ms at the median against 104 and 281, were pitched about 1.55 to 1.77 kHz against 1.46 to 1.70, and centred at 4.0 to 4.4 kHz against 3.3 to 4.0. For a flock flying over, the model's level rises about 18 dB and falls 13 against a recording of corellas flying past (9 and 18), and crosses the stereo field as it goes; no recording of lorikeets flying over was found under an open licence, so the passing shape was fitted to the corellas.
